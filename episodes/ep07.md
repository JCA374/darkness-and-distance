# Episode 7: Evian

[grave] [[Victor]] I discovered no trace of him and was beginning to conjecture that some fortunate chance had intervened to prevent the execution of his menaces when suddenly I heard a shrill and dreadful scream.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, Henry Clerval was found strangled on an Irish beach. Victor was arrested, raved for two months that he was the murderer, and was cleared by a grand jury because he'd been in the Orkneys. Days before Henry died, the creature had told Victor, "I shall be with you on your wedding-night."

This episode is about Elizabeth Lavenza. And it's the episode where I have to test the one theory that could still make all of this ordinary: that Victor did it.

---

Let's start with her, because she deserves it.

Elizabeth came into the Frankenstein house as a small child, an orphan from the shores of Lake Como. She grew up as Victor's "more than sister". When his mother was dying, it was Elizabeth's hand she joined to Victor's. After that, Elizabeth ran the household and raised the younger boys.

You've heard her already in this series. In episode two, she's the one who examined William's neck and cried out that she'd killed him, because she'd let him wear the miniature. In episode three, she's the only person in a hostile court who stood up and defended Justine Moritz, and the one who went to see her in prison, taking Victor with her. Every time this story asks someone to be brave, it's Elizabeth.

When Victor and his father reached Paris on the way home, a letter from Elizabeth caught up with them. It's dated the eighteenth of May. It's the most direct thing anyone writes to Victor in the whole file. She reminds him that their families always planned for them to marry. And then she writes, "Answer me, I conjure you by our mutual happiness, with simple truth—Do you not love another?"

She tells him that if he's only marrying her out of duty, she'd rather he didn't. That she loves him. And that she wants him to be happy, even if it isn't with her.

---

Victor's answer is important for our case, so I want you to hear how he made the decision. He says he thought about the creature's threat, and that he understood it as a threat to his own life. And he reasoned it through. If the creature meant to kill him on his wedding night, then delaying the wedding would only give it time to find "other and perhaps more dreadful means of revenge". So he decided not to delay at all.

He wrote back to Elizabeth. He told her he had a secret.

[grave] [[Victor]] I have one secret, Elizabeth, a dreadful one; when revealed to you, it will chill your frame with horror, and then, far from being surprised at my misery, you will only wonder that I survive what I have endured. I will confide this tale of misery and terror to you the day after our marriage shall take place.

The day after. Hold onto that.

Victor came home. He says Elizabeth was thinner and quieter than before. He says that, at times in those weeks, "a real insanity possessed me". The wedding was fixed for ten days later. And Victor armed himself. He says he carried pistols and a dagger "constantly about me".

---

They were married in Geneva. After the ceremony there was a large party at his father's house, and then the couple left by boat on the lake, to spend the night at Evian, on the south shore, and go on the next day to a villa Elizabeth had inherited by Lake Como.

Victor says that on her wedding day Elizabeth was melancholy, and that his father took it for a bride's nerves. They landed at Evian at eight in the evening. They walked on the shore, then went to the inn.

[grave] The wind rose in the west. A storm of rain came down. And Victor, he says, kept one hand on a pistol hidden in his clothes.

[grave] Elizabeth noticed. She asked him what was frightening him. He told her, "this night, and all will be safe; but this night is dreadful, very dreadful." After an hour, he asked her to go to bed. He would join her, he said, once he knew where his enemy was.

So Victor walked up and down the passages of the inn, checking every corner. He found nothing. He'd begun to think the creature might not come.

[grave] And then, the scream from the top of this episode. It came from the room Elizabeth had gone to. It came again. He ran in.

[grave] [[Victor]] She was there, lifeless and inanimate, thrown across the bed, her head hanging down and her pale and distorted features half covered by her hair.

[grave] He fainted. When he came to, the people of the inn were around him. Someone had moved her, and covered her face and neck with a handkerchief. He went back in.

[grave] [[Victor]] The murderous mark of the fiend's grasp was on her neck, and the breath had ceased to issue from her lips.

---

[grave] Then, Victor says, he looked up. The shutters, which had been closed, were open. The moon was shining in. And at the open window there was a figure. He says it grinned, and pointed at Elizabeth's body. He drew his pistol and fired. It leapt away, ran, and plunged into the lake.

The shot brought a crowd. They went out in boats. They cast nets. Nothing. They searched the woods and the vineyards in parties. Nothing.

And here is the line I've been waiting six episodes to read you.

[grave] [[Victor]] After passing several hours, we returned hopeless, most of my companions believing it to have been a form conjured up by my fancy.

Most of the people who were there, that night, at Evian, didn't believe there was anyone at the window.

I asked Judith whether Victor had touched that page.

[[Judith]] I looked specially, because I knew you'd ask. There's a correction two lines further down, where he's changed one word about the search parties. So he read that page with his pen in his hand. And he left that sentence exactly as Robert wrote it. Most of my companions believing it to have been a form conjured up by my fancy. He could have struck it out. He didn't.

I find that hard to read past. A man who edited this statement, who could have made himself look better on any page he chose, left in the line that says the people at the inn thought he was imagining things.

---

So let's do it properly. Let's make the case against Victor Frankenstein, as strongly as I can.

He is the only person who has ever claimed to see this creature in Geneva, in Ingolstadt, or at Evian. In each case he was alone, at night, in a state of great agitation. He had two long breakdowns with fever and delirium, one in Ingolstadt and one in Ireland, and in the second one he called himself the murderer of William, Justine and Henry, over and over, for two months. He says a "real insanity" took hold of him in the weeks before his wedding. He was taking laudanum every night on the journey home. He sat in Justine's court and said nothing. He called himself "the true murderer", though he explained he meant in effect, not in deed. He told his bride he had a terrible secret he'd reveal the day after the wedding, and she died before the day after came.

And at Evian, unlike the other two deaths, he has no alibi. He was in the building. He'd sent her to the room himself. He was armed. And the only witness to the figure at the window is him.

I asked Dr Okafor whether the mark on Elizabeth's neck tells us anything about who made it.

[[Okafor]] No. And I want to be firm about that, because people want it to. A hand at the throat leaves marks. A husband's hand would leave marks. Victor's description is "the murderous mark of the fiend's grasp", and that's not a medical observation, that's a man who's already decided who did it. From what's written down, I can say she was strangled by hand. That's all.

---

So, is it Victor?

Here's where I've landed, and I'll tell you how I got there.

If Victor killed Elizabeth, then either he also killed William and Henry, or someone else killed them with the same method and he copied it. He can't have killed Henry: an Irish grand jury, with Mr Kirwin collecting the witnesses, found he was in the Orkneys. And he very probably didn't kill William: he says he was on a walking tour around Ingolstadt with Henry, and his father's letter fits that. Three people killed the same way, with their throats grasped by hand, and the man suspected of the third was cleared of one of the others by strangers, and has a good account of where he was for the other.

[[Okafor]] I said it last time. Same signature. When I see the same method three times, in three countries, I'm looking for one person. And it's not the one a grand jury cleared.

Then there's the barn. Justine told the court, and a clerk wrote down, that steps woke her that night. Whoever that was, it wasn't Victor, who by his account was hundreds of miles away with Henry.

And then there's the ice. The sailors who saw a giant on a sledge, the day before Victor turned up. None of them had ever met him.

So no. I don't think Victor killed Elizabeth. I tried as hard as I could to make that theory work, because it would let the world stay the size I thought it was. It doesn't work. I think he's the worst witness in this case, and possibly the most tragic, and that's not the same thing. But I'll say this: if all we had was Evian, I'd be looking at the husband. Everybody would. Most of the people at the inn that night were.

And I haven't played you the one piece of evidence that settles it for me. It's not in Victor's statement. It's in the last of Walton's letters. Next week.

---

There's one more thing from this night that I can't make fit, and I'll flag it now so you can carry it with you. Victor says Elizabeth screamed. Twice. Nobody else in the file says so. The people of the inn only arrive after he's fainted. But it's what he says, and he had no reason to invent a scream. Remember that. It's going to matter.

---

Here's what happened after Evian.

[grave] Victor went back to Geneva. His father and Ernest were alive. But when Victor told his father that Elizabeth was dead, Alphonse Frankenstein went to bed and didn't get up. Within a few days he died in Victor's arms. That's the fifth death.

And then, Victor says, he lost his reason, and Geneva locked him up.

[grave] [[Victor]] For they had called me mad, and during many months, as I understood, a solitary cell had been my habitation.

About a month after he was released, he did, finally, what he'd refused to do at Justine's trial. He went to a criminal judge in Geneva and made a full statement. The creature, the making, the murders. All of it.

[[Reber]] This is the same city. Possibly the same court building. And this time the accuser is the son of a former syndic, giving a formal accusation. I have looked very hard for any trace of it. There is nothing. If the judge wrote anything down, it did not survive, or it was filed as something else. Which, given what Victor says the judge thought of it, would not surprise me.

Victor says the judge listened, and shuddered, and seemed almost to believe him. Until Victor asked him to act. Then, Victor says, the judge told him the creature would be impossible to catch, and treated the whole thing as "the effects of delirium". He was, Victor says, soothed "as a nurse does a child".

So, after five deaths, the Republic of Geneva still had Justine Moritz in its register as William's murderer, and a madman's story on file, at best, about the rest.

And Victor Frankenstein walked out of that judge's house with one plan left. He was going to find the creature himself.

Next time, the last episode. The chase. The ship. And the last witness.

This has been Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.
