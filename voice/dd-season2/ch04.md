# Episode 4: The Ravings of a Madman

[grave] [[Victor]] A thousand times rather would I have confessed myself guilty of the crime ascribed to Justine, but I was absent when it was committed, and such a declaration would have been considered as the ravings of a madman and would not have exculpated her who suffered through me.

Victor Frankenstein, on the morning a young woman went on trial in Geneva for the murder of his little brother. He says he wanted to confess. He says he didn't, because he was absent. And because he'd be taken for a madman.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance, season two. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, the first death. William Frankenstein, on the seventh of May, found on Plainpalais with the print of fingers on his neck. Victor's alibi rests on his own word. The giant he saw in the lightning rests on his own word. And Victor himself describes that giant as "my own spirit let loose from the grave".

This week, the trial of Justine Moritz. I've told this part once before, last season, as the story of an innocent woman. I'm going to tell it again. This time I'm watching Victor.

---

First, Justine, because she deserves to be more than a name in someone else's story.

Justine came to live with the Frankenstein family when she was twelve. Her own mother, a widow, treated her badly, and Victor's mother, Caroline, persuaded her to let the girl come to them. Elizabeth, in a letter to Victor, says Justine wasn't really treated as a servant. She was taught. She was loved. She copied Caroline's phrases and manners so closely that Elizabeth says she reminded her of her. When Caroline died, Justine took it very hard. Then she went home to her mother for a while, and came back to the house that winter. William was her favourite. She'd been a kind of second mother to him.

[grave] On the night William disappeared, Justine was out. By morning, she was ill. She kept to her bed for days. And then a servant, going through the clothes Justine had been wearing, found the miniature of Caroline in her pocket.

---

Dr Anselm Reber is a legal historian in Geneva. Last year he found Justine's trial file in the state archives. I went back this spring, and we read it again together, in the same reading room with the same tall windows, and this time I wasn't reading it for Justine. I was reading it for Victor.

[[Reber]] The trial is at eleven in the morning, in the old town. The procedure of the day is not a jury as you would know it. The judges hear the witnesses, and they vote. And you have to understand, Ms Lind, this is circumstantial evidence. Nobody saw her do anything. The case is that she was out all night, that she was seen near the place, that she gave a confused answer, and that she had the jewel.

The witnesses lined up. Justine had been out the whole night. Towards morning a market-woman saw her not far from where the body was found, and asked her what she was doing there, and got a strange, confused answer. She came home at about eight. When she was shown the body, she fell into hysterics. Then the miniature was produced. Elizabeth was called, and in a faltering voice she said it was the same picture she'd hung round William's neck an hour before he went missing. Victor says "a murmur of horror and indignation filled the court."

Then Justine spoke in her own defence. She'd spent that evening with an aunt in Chêne, a village about a league from Geneva. Walking back at about nine, she met a man who asked if she'd seen a lost child. She searched for hours. The gates of Geneva shut. She spent the rest of the night in a barn belonging to a cottage, because she didn't want to wake the family who lived there. She watched most of the night. Near morning, she thinks she slept a few minutes.

Reber turned the file round and pointed. This is the clerk's note of her defence, in the court's own record.

[[Archive]] The accused says that she passed the night in a barn, that she was woken by steps, and that she cannot account for the portrait.

[[Reber]] Woken by steps. The clerk wrote it down. In the procedure of the day, he writes what matters to the defence. I underlined it last year, and I underline it again.

Justine told the court she had no enemy on earth. And then she asked a question out loud, in that courtroom, in front of everyone. Did the murderer put it there? She said she couldn't see how, or why he'd steal a jewel only to give it up again so soon.

---

Now, here's why I'm telling you all of this again. Where was Victor?

Victor was in the room. He was sitting with his family. He heard every word of that defence. The aunt at Chêne. The man who asked about the lost child. The closed gates. A barn belonging to a cottage. A young woman alone there all night. A few minutes' sleep near dawn. Steps. And a portrait she couldn't account for.

Keep every one of those details. You're going to hear them again next week, from somebody else.

The character witnesses were frightened, and said very little. Then Elizabeth asked to speak, and she spoke for Justine with everything she had. She said Justine had nursed Caroline in her last illness. She said Justine adored William. She said that if Justine had wanted the jewel, she, Elizabeth, would have given it to her gladly.

And Victor? Here's what he tells Walton about his own state in that courtroom.

[[Victor]] Could the daemon who had (I did not for a minute doubt) murdered my brother also in his hellish sport have betrayed the innocent to death and ignominy? I could not sustain the horror of my situation, and when I perceived that the popular voice and the countenances of the judges had already condemned my unhappy victim, I rushed out of the court in agony.

My unhappy victim. He means Justine. He calls her his victim.

---

I played that passage to Dr Kessler, and then the one I opened with. I'd assumed she'd focus on the confession he didn't make. She didn't.

[[Kessler]] Listen to the reason he gives for not speaking. Not that it was untrue. He says it would be considered the ravings of a madman. He comes back to that over and over, doesn't he? Madman. Delirium. Ravings of insanity. In the space of a few days he's raised the possibility that he's mad three or four times, and each time it's because of how other people would see him. He never once seems to ask it of himself. That's not unusual, by the way. It's very hard to look at your own mind from the outside.

The next morning Victor went back to the court. He couldn't bring himself to ask. But an officer knew who he was, and told him.

[grave] [[Victor]] The ballots had been thrown; they were all black, and Justine was condemned.

The officer added something. Justine had confessed. He said the judges were glad of it, because none of them liked to condemn on circumstantial evidence alone.

And Victor, standing there, asks himself a question. I'd never noticed it before this year. "Had my eyes deceived me? And was I really as mad as the whole world would believe me to be if I disclosed the object of my suspicions?"

[[Kessler]] There it is. Once. He asks it once.

---

That afternoon, Justine asked to see Elizabeth. Elizabeth said she'd go, and that Victor would have to come with her, because she couldn't go alone.

I wanted to know if that visit was on file. Reber thought it might be. A prisoner under sentence of death was watched, and the prison kept a book. It took us the rest of the day, but here it is.

[[Archive]] Justine Moritz, condemned. Visited by Mademoiselle Lavenza and by Monsieur Frankenstein, the son.

That's the Geneva prison's own book. It puts Victor in that cell, and it agrees with his account, and I want to give him credit for that. Every time I've been able to check where Victor says he was, he was there.

Justine was sitting on straw at the far end of the cell. Her hands were in chains. She threw herself at Elizabeth's feet. Elizabeth asked her why she'd confessed. And Justine told her. "I did confess, but I confessed a lie." Her confessor had besieged her, she said, and threatened her with excommunication and hell fire. And then she says a sentence I can't get out of my head this season.

[grave] She says the confessor threatened her "until I almost began to think that I was the monster that he said I was."

The monster that he said I was. Spoken by the one person in this story we know was innocent of everything. That's how easily it happens, she's saying. Tell someone often enough, and they'll start to believe they're the monster.

---

While the two women talked, Victor stood in a corner of the cell. He says he gnashed his teeth and groaned. Justine heard him, and came over, and thanked him for coming. She asked if he believed she was guilty. He couldn't answer. Elizabeth answered for him. He believes you, she said.

And then Victor says this.

[[Victor]] I, the true murderer, felt the never-dying worm alive in my bosom, which allowed of no hope or consolation.

A few weeks later, at home, while Elizabeth grieved, he says it again: "I, not in deed, but in effect, was the true murderer."

So here's what I take from the courtroom. Victor sat through that whole trial. He heard every detail of Justine's night in the barn, told by her, recorded by a clerk, in front of a crowd. Whatever story he told later about what happened in that barn, he didn't need anyone else to tell it to him. He'd heard it at eleven in the morning, in the old town of Geneva.

And he called himself the true murderer. Twice. In deed, he said, no. In effect, yes. On the theory, the line between those two is exactly the line he couldn't see.

---

Here's the thing that nags me. Woken by steps.

Justine said that in her own defence, before Victor had told anybody anything. If she was alone in that barn, what woke her? On Dr Kessler's theory, the person who put the portrait in her clothes was Victor. But by his own account, he was in Bavaria that night, and nothing we have puts him in that barn. And Justine heard steps.

Dr Kessler didn't try to wave it away.

[[Kessler]] Either somebody was in that barn or she dreamed it. People do wake to sounds that aren't there, especially when they've slept a few minutes in a frightening night. I can't tell you which. But I'll say this. If someone was there, then on this theory either Victor wasn't where he says he was, or Victor wasn't alone. Neither of those is in the documents. That's a door I can't close.

Neither can I. Somebody put that portrait in Justine's clothes. Either Victor, or someone with him, or something else.

---

[grave] Justine Moritz died the next morning. Elizabeth and Victor both appealed to the judges, and the judges sent them cold answers. Victor says his confession "died away on my lips". "Thus I might proclaim myself a madman," he says, "but not revoke the sentence passed upon my wretched victim." She was executed as a murderer. In the city's register, last time I looked, she still is.

[grave] Victor says that afterwards he couldn't sleep. He rowed out alone on the lake at night. He thought about drowning himself. He thought about the creature. And he says he wanted to meet it again, so he could avenge William and Justine.

That summer, he got his wish. On a glacier above Chamounix, in the Alps, he says the thing he made came striding towards him across the ice, and asked him to sit down and listen.

What it said is the next part of the letters. In the creature's own voice, as Victor told it, and as Victor later corrected it, in his own hand.
