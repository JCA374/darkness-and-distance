# Episode 3: My Own Spirit

[grave] [[Victor]] I considered the being whom I had cast among mankind, and endowed with the will and power to effect purposes of horror, such as the deed which he had now done, nearly in the light of my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave, and forced to destroy all that was dear to me.

Victor Frankenstein, describing a night he spent in the rain, on a plain outside Geneva, after his little brother was murdered there. My own vampire. My own spirit, let loose from the grave.

He wrote that. Or he said it, and a sea captain wrote it down, and then Victor read it over and let it stand.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance, season two. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, Ingolstadt. Two years of work with the dead, a November night, and a figure at the bed that only Victor saw. Then a winter of fever, and Victor raving about a monster to his friend Henry Clerval, who never saw it. Then two quiet years. Then a letter.

This week, the first death. And the first time Victor gives the thing he saw a crime.

---

The letter was from Victor's father, Alphonse, and it was waiting for Victor in Ingolstadt when he and Henry came back from a walking tour. Here's what it says happened.

It was a Thursday evening in May, the seventh. Warm and calm. Alphonse, who had served as a syndic, one of the city's chief magistrates; Victor's cousin Elizabeth; and Victor's brothers, Ernest and William, went for a walk on Plainpalais, a broad public walk just outside the city walls of Geneva. The boys ran ahead. William hid, the way small boys do, and Ernest couldn't find him. They searched until dark, then with torches. At five in the morning, his father found him.

[grave] [[Victor]] About five in the morning I discovered my lovely boy, whom the night before I had seen blooming and active in health, stretched on the grass livid and motionless; the print of the murder's finger was on his neck.

William was the youngest. A few months earlier, Elizabeth had described him for Victor in a letter. Tall for his age, she says. "Sweet laughing blue eyes, dark eyelashes, and curling hair." Dimples when he smiles. He already has one or two little wives, she writes, and his favourite is a girl called Louisa Biron, who is five.

[grave] That evening, William had begged Elizabeth to let him wear something precious. A miniature, a tiny portrait, of his dead mother, Caroline. Elizabeth said yes. When they carried him home, the miniature was gone. And when Elizabeth saw the marks on his neck, she cried out that she had murdered him, because she'd let him wear it.

Every time I read that passage, I stop for a moment on Louisa Biron, who was five. Somebody had to tell her.

---

So. Dr Kessler's question. Who else saw it?

For William's death, the first thing the theory has to deal with is where Victor was. Because if he was in Bavaria, he can't have been on Plainpalais.

Here's what we have. Victor says that in early May, he and Henry went on a walking tour around Ingolstadt. A fortnight, in the countryside, the two of them. They came back on a Sunday afternoon, and the letter was waiting. The murder was on a Thursday, the seventh. Alphonse wrote on the twelfth. Victor says he got it on the Sunday after.

That's an alibi. A good one, if you can check it. So I tried.

I spent most of this month on it. I went back to Munich, to the university papers. A walking tour in early May leaves no trace in a university. No lecture lists, no notes. I asked Dr Anselm Reber, the legal historian in Geneva who has been working with me since last season, whether the Clerval family papers might help. Last year he found the notary's record of Henry's estate. This year he went back through the family's letters.

[[Reber]] There is nothing. I want to be clear what that means. It does not mean the walk did not happen. It means that a fortnight of two young men walking in the Bavarian countryside left no paper that has survived. That is normal. On the documents, the only witness to where Victor Frankenstein was on the seventh of May is Victor Frankenstein.

That's a dead end, and I want to be honest that it's a dead end both ways. Nothing puts him in Geneva. Nothing puts him in Bavaria, except his word.

And his word says Henry was there.

---

Victor left Ingolstadt at once. He says he travelled slowly. He stopped two days at Lausanne, too upset to go on. He reached Geneva after dark, when the gates were already shut, so he spent the night at Secheron, a village outside the walls on the lake.

He couldn't sleep. He decided to visit the place where William died. And on the way, a storm came in over the mountains.

Victor crossed the lake by boat to get there. I walked the shore last season instead, at night, in light rain, about forty minutes. Victor describes the storm like nothing else in his statement: lightning on Mont Blanc, thunder from every side, the lake lit up "like a vast sheet of fire". He walks into it, and he calls out loud to his dead brother: "William, dear angel! this is thy funeral, this thy dirge!"

[grave] [[Victor]] As I said these words, I perceived in the gloom a figure which stole from behind a clump of trees near me; I stood fixed, gazing intently: I could not be mistaken. A flash of lightning illuminated the object, and discovered its shape plainly to me; its gigantic stature, and the deformity of its aspect more hideous than belongs to humanity, instantly informed me that it was the wretch, the filthy daemon, to whom I had given life.

Victor has the thought: could this be William's murderer? And the moment he thinks it, he's sure. I want to read you the next line exactly, because last year I skipped right over it.

[[Victor]] He was the murderer! I could not doubt it. The mere presence of the idea was an irresistible proof of the fact.

I read that to Dr Kessler. She asked me to read it again.

[[Kessler]] The mere presence of the idea was an irresistible proof of the fact. That's a remarkable sentence. He's telling you how he knows. He knows because he thought it. In my old work, that's the sentence you'd circle in red. Not because it's mad, but because it's honest. It tells you that, for Victor, thinking something and knowing it had become the same act.

Then another flash, and Victor sees the figure again, already high up on the Salêve, the long wall of rock that stands over the south of the city. It reaches the top, and it's gone.

I've stood on that plain and looked at the Salêve. Even with the cable car, it takes a while.

---

Victor stays out all night in the rain. And this is where he says the thing I opened with. He thinks about what he made, and he says he saw it "nearly in the light of my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave".

I asked Dr Kessler whether I was making too much of a figure of speech.

[[Kessler]] Possibly. It's a period phrase, and he's a man who talks in big phrases. But let's look at what it says. My own spirit. Let loose from the grave. Forced to destroy all that was dear to me. He's describing something that is his, that came out of the dead, and that is compelled to harm the people he loves. Forced. Not choosing. If you asked me to write down, in one sentence, what this theory is, I couldn't do much better than Victor did.

Here's why I take it seriously. A few hours later, by his own account, Victor tells us two more things about himself. That morning he decides to say nothing to his family about the figure. And he explains why. He remembers the fever in Ingolstadt, at the very time he dated the creation, and he says it "would give an air of delirium to a tale otherwise so utterly improbable". He says that if anyone else told him this story, he'd take it for "the ravings of insanity".

So the man himself saw the problem, on the first morning. He saw how it would sound to others, and he kept silent.

---

I wanted one more opinion, and this one doesn't care about Victor's mind at all. Dr Paul Okafor is a retired forensic pathologist. Last season I asked him about the marks on William's neck.

This year I asked him something more uncomfortable. Could Victor have done it?

[[Okafor]] Physically? Of course. William was a small child. The print of a finger on the neck means pressure by a hand. It doesn't take a large hand, and it doesn't take long. I'm sorry. Any adult could have done it. A man, a woman, a giant, a student. The body tells you that someone put their hands on his throat. It doesn't tell you whose. That's what I said last year and it's still all I can say.

I asked him whether anything in the letters points to a hand larger than a man's.

[[Okafor]] Not in this death. Nobody measured the marks. Nobody said they were enormous. If there's evidence of a giant here, it's in what Victor saw on the plain afterwards, not on the boy.

---

So here's where the theory stands, after the first death. Victor's alibi rests on his word. The figure on the plain rests on his word. And Victor's own description of it, my own spirit let loose from the grave, fits the theory like a glove.

But there's a problem, and his name is Henry.

By Victor's account, Henry Clerval was with him for the whole fortnight around the seventh of May, walking in Bavaria. That's not a detail Victor needed. If you're inventing an alibi, you don't name a witness who's alive, who comes from your own city, and who knows your family. Henry stayed his friend, and years later traveled with him to England and Scotland. He was the friend who nursed him, who knew him better than anyone. And nowhere, in anything we have, does anyone say Henry Clerval was troubled about where Victor had been that week.

Dr Kessler's answer was careful.

[[Kessler]] We don't have Henry's words. We have Victor's account of Henry. So Henry can't contradict anything, because Henry never speaks except through Victor. I'd also say this. If the theory is right, Victor might sincerely believe he was walking in the hills. That's what makes it a theory about illness, and not about lying. But you're right that it's a strain. I'll grant you the strain.

So I'm marking it the way Reber marks things. On the documents, where Victor was on the seventh of May is open. And so far, it stays open.

---

[grave] At five in the morning, the city gates opened, and Victor walked into his father's house, soaked, having seen what he'd seen. He told no one.

His brother Ernest came down. Ernest had news. Victor didn't need to worry about the murderer, Ernest said. The family had already found out who it was.

[grave] The missing miniature had turned up. A servant had found it in the pocket of the dress worn that night by a young woman who had lived in the Frankenstein house since she was twelve. Her name was Justine Moritz. She'd been arrested.

And Victor, who says he knew the truth, was about to sit in a courtroom and watch her tried for her life.
