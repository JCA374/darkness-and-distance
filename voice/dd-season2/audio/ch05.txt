That's the voice of the thing Victor Frankenstein says he made, describing the moment after it killed his brother. [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) confessed to this murder and took it back. It reaches us through Victor's memory, Walton's pen, and then Victor's pen again.

Clapping my hands. Hold onto that. Victor clapped his hands too, once, on a morning in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/).

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance, season two. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, the trial of [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) Moritz. Victor sat in the court and heard her whole defence. A night in a barn, a few minutes' sleep near dawn, steps that woke her, a portrait she couldn't explain. He called himself the true murderer. And she went to the scaffold.

This week, the statement of the suspect. The creature's own account of its life, and of the night William died. It's the longest single voice in Victor's whole story. And this week, for the first time, I'm going to tell you what the conservator found in the ink.

[pause 1.5]

It's the middle of August, about two months after [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) died. Victor has suddenly left home, alone, and gone up into the Alps, to the valley of [Chamounix](/ʃæmunˈi/), to try to forget himself. One morning he climbs alone to [Montanvert](/mɑntɑnvˈɛɹ/), to look out over the glacier they call the sea of ice. He says he went without a guide, deliberately, because "the presence of another would destroy the solitary grandeur of the scene".

Alone, again.

He crosses the ice. It takes him nearly two hours. He sits on a rock. He calls out loud to the wandering spirits of the dead to take him with them. And then he sees a figure, at a distance, coming towards him across the glacier, bounding over the crevasses "with superhuman speed".

It's the creature. Victor attacks it. It steps aside easily. It asks him to listen. And it says something I've thought about a lot since Dr Kessler first wrote to me.

[[Creature]] Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed.

They go into a mountain hut. The creature lights a fire. And it talks, Victor says, until the sun goes down.

[pause 1.5]

I'll give you the story in outline, because it's long, and because it's beautiful, and I don't want to rush it.

The creature says that after it left Victor's rooms in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), it wandered in the forest, cold, confused, unable to speak. It was driven out of a village with stones. It found a lean-to built against the wall of a cottage, and hid there for months. Through a crack in the wall, it watched the family who lived there. A blind old man named De Lacey. His son Felix and his daughter Agatha. Exiles from France, living in poverty.

It learned to speak by listening to them. When a young Arabian woman, [Safie](/sˈɑfi/), came to live with them, and Felix taught her French, the creature learned along with her. It found books in the woods and taught itself to read. It learned, from overheard conversation, how they had all come to ruin in Paris. [Safie](/sˈɑfi/)'s father, a Turkish merchant, was condemned to death in an unjust trial, and Felix helped him escape.

And it learned it was hideous. It saw its reflection in a pool.

It decided to show itself to the blind father, alone, because a blind man couldn't be frightened by its face. The old man was kind to it. Then Felix came in, and beat it with a stick. The family left the cottage for ever. That night, the creature burned it down.

It set out for Geneva, to find its maker. On the way, it pulled a girl out of a river, and the man with her shot it in the shoulder.

[grave] And then, near Geneva, at evening, a little boy ran into its hiding place.

[pause 1.5]

[grave] [[Creature]] I grasped his throat to silence him, and in a moment he lay dead at my feet.

The creature says it saw something glittering on the boy's breast. A portrait of a beautiful woman. It took it. And then it went looking for a better place to hide.

[[Creature]] I entered a barn which had appeared to me to be empty. A woman was sleeping on some straw; she was young, not indeed so beautiful as her whose portrait I held, but of an agreeable aspect and blooming in the loveliness of youth and health.

It whispers to her. She stirs. And it's afraid that she'll wake and see it and denounce it. Then this.

[grave] [[Creature]] The thought was madness; it stirred the fiend within me, not I, but she, shall suffer; the murder I have committed because I am for ever robbed of all that she could give me, she shall atone.

[[Creature]] I bent over her and placed the portrait securely in one of the folds of her dress. She moved again, and I fled.

[pause 1.5]

I read the barn passage to Dr Kessler with the trial file open beside me. Then I did what she'd asked me to do in her very first letter. I made two columns.

On the left, what the court heard, with Victor sitting in it. The portrait was on William's neck, Elizabeth said so. [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) spent the night in a barn belonging to a cottage. She was alone. She slept a little, near dawn. Something disturbed her. She couldn't account for the portrait.

On the right, what the creature says. Something glittering on the boy's breast. A barn. A young woman, alone, asleep on straw. She stirred. The portrait, placed on her.

Nearly every detail on the right is on the left.

[[Kessler]] Which is exactly what you'd expect if it's true, of course. I want to say that first. A true account and a borrowed one look the same when they agree. What I notice is something else. Three things the creature says aren't in the court. The straw she slept on. The words it whispers, which nobody could check. And where the portrait went. The court said her pocket. The creature says the folds of her dress.

I asked her which way that cuts.

[[Kessler]] Both ways, honestly. If you're copying the trial, why change the pocket? That argues for an independent witness. But if you're a mind telling yourself a story about the barn, you don't copy. You remember. And memory moves things around. I can't settle it. I'd only ask you to notice that the creature's words, at the worst moment, are "it stirred the fiend within me". Within me. It is describing being two.

Now the ink.

Remember what Walton says. Victor found out that Walton was making notes, and asked to see them. Then he "corrected and augmented them in many places, but principally in giving the life and spirit to the conversations he held with his enemy." I read you the first half of that line in episode one. I left out the word principally.

This spring, the conservator went through every page of Victor's statement in Judith's letters. She marked every place where the second hand and the second ink appear: scraped words, words written over, lines added between lines. Then she counted. I asked her to give me the count by voice. Whose words were they in?

Judith called me the night the report came. She'd read it twice before she rang.

[[Judith]] I'll be honest, Tessa, I didn't like it. Robert says it in his own letter, mind you, so it oughtn't to be a surprise. But it's different to see it in a table.

Here is the conservator's summary, as it's written in her report.

[[Archive]] Second hand and ink, the stranger's own narrative: light, scattered. Second hand and ink, the speeches of the being he describes: heavy, on almost every page.

[grave] And then a list of the passages where the second ink is heaviest. The glacier. The cottage. The child. And the barn.

So when you heard the creature say "it stirred the fiend within me", you were hearing a passage the conservator lists among the heaviest for Victor's ink.

[pause 1.5]

I want to be very careful here, and Dr Kessler was more careful than me.

[[Kessler]] It tells you where he worked. It doesn't tell you what he changed. He might have fixed a word Walton misheard. He might have rewritten whole speeches. We don't know, and the conservator rightly won't guess. But here's what I'd say in court, if I had to. The voice of the creature, as we have it, is the voice Victor wanted to leave behind. The most eloquent speaker in this whole document, the one who reads Milton and argues like a philosopher, is the one Victor spent the most ink on. And Walton tells his sister, more than once, how powerful Victor's eloquence was.

Here's what I believe about this part, and why. Last season I told you the creature's statement was the suspect's own story. This season I have to say it plainer. It's the suspect's own story as told by Victor, and then as corrected by Victor, and the conservator can show you on the page where. On the theory, those aren't two voices. They're one man, writing both sides of a conversation with himself, at the end of his life, in another man's notebook.

The creature clapped its hands over William's body. In [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), the morning after the creation, Victor clapped his hands, and jumped over the chairs, and laughed a laugh that frightened his best friend. I'm not saying that proves anything. I'm saying I noticed it, and once you notice it you can't stop.

[pause 1.5]

And now the part that pushes back. It's a big one.

The creature told Victor it had copies of letters. Letters between Felix and [Safie](/sˈɑfi/), which it said it copied out while it lived in the hovel. "Before I depart I will give them to you," it says. "They will prove the truth of my tale."

And Victor had them. Walton saw them. Walton's own letter says that "the letters of Felix and [Safie](/sˈɑfi/), which he showed me," convinced him more than anything Victor said. Those letters were real paper, in Walton's hands, on the ship.

And last season, in the Paris police papers, I found the De Lacey family. A Turkish merchant condemned to death and escaping the night before his execution. A family named De Lacey arrested and banished for it. Exactly what the creature describes. Real people, in a real archive.

So I asked Dr Kessler the obvious question. If Victor is the only one there, how did he know the De Laceys?

[[Kessler]] I don't know. And I want to be clear that I don't know. The theory would need Victor to have come across that family, or their papers, somewhere in the two years after the November night, and to have folded them into his story. Nothing in the documents says he did. The other answer is that somebody else gave him those letters. Which is the creature's answer. Or a third person's. I'm a psychiatrist. I can explain a man's mind. I can't explain a bundle of letters he couldn't have written.

Somebody copied those letters out. Either the creature did, in its hovel, as it says. Or someone we've never met did. Or Victor came across them somewhere, which nothing shows. I'm leaving that one open. I don't have a choice.

[grave] At the end of the day in the hut, the creature makes its demand. It's alone, it says, and it will always be alone, and that's what made it cruel. It wants Victor to make it a companion. A female, of the same kind. If he does, the two of them will go to the wilds of South America and never be seen again.

Victor refuses. Then the creature talks some more. And Victor, astonishingly, agrees.

He doesn't start at home. He tells his family he needs to go to England, for his studies. Henry [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/) goes with him. They travel through London, Oxford, the Lake District, Scotland. And then Victor leaves Henry behind, in Perth, and goes on alone, to the most remote place he can find. An island in the Orkneys, almost bare rock, with five people living on it.
