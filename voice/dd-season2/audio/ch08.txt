When he was released, Victor says, the first thing he felt was the wish for revenge. About a month later, he went to a criminal judge in Geneva and told him everything. The creation. The glacier. William, [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/), Henry, Elizabeth. He asked the judge to arrest the creature.

Dr [Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/) has read a great many Genevan magistrates, and he says this part of Victor's statement rings very true.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] Look at what the judge does. He listens. He is polite. Victor says he gave the story "that half kind of belief that is given to a tale of spirits." And then, when he is asked to act, he finds a reason not to. The creature could be anywhere, he says. It could cross the ice and live in caves. It is months since the crimes. This is a magistrate who has just been told a story by a man recently released from confinement, and who does not wish to say so to his face.

Victor tells us what happened next. The judge, he says, tried to soothe him "as a nurse does a child," and treated his whole account as "the effects of delirium."

So Victor left Geneva for good. Of his whole family, only his brother Ernest was still alive. The letters never tell us what became of Ernest. He took some money and his mother's jewels. On his last night, he went to the cemetery where William and Elizabeth and his father were buried. He knelt on the grass and swore to hunt down the thing that killed them. And as he finished, he says, he heard a laugh.

[grave] [[Victor]] I was answered through the stillness of night by a loud and fiendish laugh. It rang on my ears long and heavily; the mountains re-echoed it, and I felt as if all hell surrounded me with mockery and laughter.

Then a voice, close to his ear, in a whisper. "I am satisfied, miserable wretch! You have determined to live, and I am satisfied."

[pause 1.5]

What follows is the chase, and Victor tells it quickly. Down the Rhone to the Mediterranean. He says he saw the creature board a ship for the Black Sea, at night, and took passage on the same ship. Then across the wilds of Tartary and Russia. Months. Then years, maybe. He doesn't say.

And all the way, he says, the creature left him signs. Marks in writing on the bark of trees, or cut in stone. Sometimes food. One inscription told him where to find a dead hare, and to eat it. "My reign is not yet over," another one said. "You live, and my power is complete."

Here's where Dr Kessler stopped me, the first time we went through it, and asked me to read a passage twice. It's Victor, describing how he was fed when he was starving in the wilderness.

[[Victor]] Sometimes, when nature, overcome by hunger, sank under the exhaustion, a repast was prepared for me in the desert that restored and inspirited me. The fare was, indeed, coarse, such as the peasants of the country ate, but I will not doubt that it was set there by the spirits that I had invoked to aid me.

[[Kessler]] He tells us himself he doesn't know where the food came from. He thinks spirits. Then a few lines later he describes his dreams, how he lived for sleep because in sleep he saw his wife and his father and his friend, and how sometimes they came to him when he was awake. And then he says this. He says he pursued the creature "as the mechanical impulse of some power of which I was unconscious."

A power of which he was unconscious. I've read this statement maybe forty times now, and I swear I never saw that line before Dr Kessler pointed at it.

[[Kessler]] On the theory, who wrote on the trees? The honest answer is, on the theory, nobody wrote on the trees, or Victor did. A man chasing himself across Russia, leaving himself messages, finding food he can't remember setting down. I'm not saying that's what happened. I'm saying that's what this paragraph describes, if you read it without the creature in it. And it reads very easily without the creature in it.

Here's the thing that nags me, on the theory. The last village.

Victor says that when he finally reached the Arctic shore, with a sledge and dogs, he came to a poor little hamlet by the sea. And the people there told him, he says, that a gigantic monster had arrived the night before, with a gun and many pistols. It had frightened a family out of their cottage, taken their winter food, seized a team of trained dogs, and gone out across the frozen sea, towards no land at all. They expected it to drown, or freeze.

That comes to us through Victor, like almost everything else. But it's other people. A whole village saw something, and was afraid of it, and lost its winter food.

And then there's the first page. Walton's crew, on the thirty-first of July. Half a mile off, through telescopes. "A being which had the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic stature." A sledge and dogs, going north. The next morning, a second sledge, with one dog left alive, and Victor inside it.

So, Walton's ship. Victor tells his story over about a week. And in the weeks after that, Walton writes to his sister about the man in his cabin, who is dying.

He says something at the end of August that, on the theory, is very hard to read. Victor, he says, believes that when he talks with his dead friends in his dreams, they are "not the creations of his fancy, but the beings themselves who visit him from the regions of a remote world."

The ice closes in. Sailors die. The crew comes to Walton in a deputation and demands that if the ice frees them, he turns for home. Victor, from his bed, gives them a speech about glory, and Walton says it moves them. On the seventh of September, Walton gives in. He promises to turn south if the ice lets them go. On the eleventh, it does.

Victor tries to get up, and faints. The surgeon says he has hours. And in his last speech, which Walton wrote down soon after, Victor says something about himself that I've never been able to hear the same way since Dr Kessler's first letter.

[grave] [[Victor]] In a fit of enthusiastic madness I created a rational creature and was bound towards him to assure, as far as was in my power, his happiness and well-being.

He asks Walton to finish his work, if he can, and destroy the creature. Then he takes it back. Then he says goodbye.

[grave] [[Walton]] His voice became fainter as he spoke, and at length, exhausted by his effort, he sank into silence. About half an hour afterwards he attempted again to speak but was unable; he pressed my hand feebly, and his eyes closed for ever, while the irradiation of a gentle smile passed away from his lips.

So here's where I've landed. In the trailer I promised you three moments this theory can't explain. Here they are, and here are the three doors they leave open.

The first door is Victor alone. It explains the fever in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/) and the figure at the bed that only he saw. It explains the giant in the lightning on [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/), that Victor himself called "my own spirit let loose from the grave". It explains how the creature's account of the barn matches what Victor heard in court, and why the creature's voice carries more of Victor's ink than his own story does.

It explains the face on the island, the confessions over Henry's body and in two months of raving, the figure at Evian that the searchers didn't believe in, the cell in Geneva, the words on the trees and the food in the desert. It explains a great deal. Dr Kessler won't say it's true, and neither will I. But last year I'd never have believed how much of this file it explains.

Then the three moments. The first page: the crew's giant, on the thirty-first of July, before anyone on that ship had heard Victor's name. The night Henry died: Victor on an island in the Orkneys, a grand jury's finding, and a man in a boat off an Irish beach. And a last page, which I've held back all season.

The second door is Victor with somebody. Not a creature built on a table. A living person, very big, perhaps badly scarred, who was there when Victor was not. Somebody who could be seen from a ship through a telescope, and row a boat to an Irish beach, and stand in a barn while a young woman slept, and copy out a family's letters.

On that door, Victor's illness didn't invent a killer. It took a real one and told a story about him: that he was made, from the dead, by Victor. None of that has a name, and none of it is in the documents. I won't make one up. But it's the door that fits the most pages at once.

The third door is the one I walked through last season. That everything Victor said was true.

The last page is the one that keeps those two doors open. It comes after Victor is dead.

Walton has just finished telling his sister that Victor is dead. He's grieving. He says he's going home to England. And then he stops writing.

[grave] [[Walton]] I am interrupted. What do these sounds portend? It is midnight; the breeze blows fairly, and the watch on deck scarcely stir. Again there is a sound as of a human voice, but hoarser; it comes from the cabin where the remains of Frankenstein still lie. I must arise and examine.

[grave] [[Walton]] I entered the cabin where lay the remains of my ill-fated and admirable friend. Over him hung a form which I cannot find words to describe, gigantic in stature, yet uncouth and distorted in its proportions. As he hung over the coffin, his face was concealed by long locks of ragged hair; but one vast hand was extended, in colour and apparent texture like that of a mummy.

It turns to the window. Walton calls on it to stay. And it speaks. Not to Victor. Victor is dead. It speaks to the body, and then to Walton.

[grave] [[Creature]] That is also my victim!

[grave] [[Creature]] In his murder my crimes are consummated; the miserable series of my being is wound to its close!

It talks for a long time. About Henry. "Think you that the groans of [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/) were music to my ears?" About Elizabeth, and the threat it made. About what it had done. And then something that, after this season, I can't hear any other way.

[[Creature]] I look on the hands which executed the deed; I think on the heart in which the imagination of it was conceived and long for the moment when these hands will meet my eyes, when that imagination will haunt my thoughts no more.

It says it will leave the ship on the ice raft that brought it there. That it will go to the far north, build its own funeral pyre, and burn. That soon, the memory of both of them will vanish.

[grave] [[Walton]] He sprang from the cabin-window as he said this, upon the ice raft which lay close to the vessel. He was soon borne away by the waves and lost in darkness and distance.

I read that last page to Dr Kessler on the phone, at the end of our last call. She was quiet for a long time.

[[Kessler]] I've spent this season explaining Victor. I can't explain that page with Victor. He was dead. Walton wrote it that night, alone, in his own hand. So what did Walton see? Read him carefully. A form, by lamplight, bent over a body, its face hidden by hair. He says, "I shut my eyes involuntarily." A moment later, "I dared not again raise my eyes to his face." He saw something enormous and terrible, in the dark, for a few minutes, mostly with his eyes closed. And it spoke of Henry, and of Victor, and of the wedding night, like someone who had been inside his story.

I asked her what that means.

[[Kessler]] It means somebody was there. Whether that somebody was made in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), or was a man who had followed Victor across the world and knew his story because he had lived through it beside him, that page can't tell us. A sick man's story and a real visitor are not opposites. I think, if I'm honest, that's where the documents leave us. Not one man. But not a monster built from the dead, either, unless you take Victor's word for it. And he's the one witness we've spent this season learning not to lean on.

[grave] So here's where I am. I believe Victor Frankenstein was ill, and I believe it more than I did a year ago, because he told us, over and over, in his own words, and the city of Geneva wrote it down. I believe that ill man saw things no one else saw, and called himself a murderer to anyone who would listen. On the night Elizabeth died, nobody can say where he was. On the night Henry died, he couldn't have been on that beach. Somebody was.

If you made me stand somewhere, I'd stand between the first two doors. A sick man, and somebody real beside him, who the sick man turned into a monster in the telling. Or who was one.

And the third door I can't lock. Because a sea captain, alone, at midnight, with his friend dead in the next cabin, heard a voice, and opened the door.

Who was in the room with Victor Frankenstein? Himself. Somebody we've never met. Or the one he made.

No body. No pyre. No name. Just a raft of ice, and a figure on it, going north, until Walton couldn't see it any more.

This has been Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.
