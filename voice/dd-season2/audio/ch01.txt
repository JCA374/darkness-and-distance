Victor Frankenstein was born into one of the leading families of Geneva. At seventeen he left for the university of [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), in Bavaria, and stayed away nearly six years. Somewhere in those years, on a night in November, he says he gave life to a body he had built from the dead. He says it fled the same night. Then, for almost two years, nothing.

Then the deaths begin, and they travel. Geneva, and a courtroom in the old town. A glacier in the Alps, where Victor says the thing he made came to him and asked to be heard. England, Scotland, one of the remotest islands of the Orkneys. A beach in Ireland. Home to Geneva, for a wedding. And at last out onto the frozen sea, where Walton's ship found him.

That's the route. I'm not going to tell you yet how it ends. Some of it, I didn't believe until I'd held the paper.

Now. The popular name for what Dr Kessler is asking about is split personality. I said those words to her on the phone, and she gave a small sigh.

[[Kessler]] That phrase. The clinical name today is dissociative identity disorder. It's rare. It's debated, even among people who treat it. And it's not what I'm claiming. What I'm saying is narrower. These letters describe a man who, by his own account, ran fevers for months, raved, saw things that weren't in the room, and lost stretches of time. And they describe a killer that almost nobody else ever saw. I'm asking whether those two might be the same story told from two sides.

So that's the theory. Victor didn't make a monster. Victor became one, and didn't know it, or knew it in some way he could only tell as a story about someone else.

I'm going to give that theory every chance this season. And I'm going to tell you, every time, when the documents push back. Because they do.

Let me take you to Kent first, because that's where the letters are.

Judith Saville is a retired schoolteacher. The letters came down in her family for two hundred years, in a sea chest, because they were written to her ancestor, Margaret Saville. Robert Walton was Margaret's brother. He wrote to her from Saint Petersburg, from Archangel, and then from a ship stuck in the ice, on a voyage to find a passage to the North Pole. Every letter is dated by month and day. The year is always written "seventeen", and a dash. We never say it.

When I told Judith about Dr Kessler's letter, she wasn't offended. She was interested.

[[Judith]] Robert would have hated it, mind you. He loved that man. But I'm a teacher. When a pupil says the book is wrong, you don't send them out of the room. You make them show you where.

So this spring, Judith did something she'd been meaning to do for years. She had the letters looked at by a paper conservator, a specialist in old documents. And there was one thing I asked her to look for, specifically.

Because here's what I knew from last season. When Victor told Walton his story, Walton wrote it down every night, "as nearly as possible in his own words". And later, Walton says, Victor asked to see the notes. And then he "corrected and augmented them in many places". The letters show it. There's a second hand, and a second ink, in the part of the letters that holds Victor's story.

So my question to the conservator was simple. Where does the second ink start? What did Victor never touch?

She started with the letter where the crew first sees something on the ice. Walton dated it August fifth. Judith read me the report over the phone, and then she sent me a photograph of the page.

Walton's ship is stuck. Ice on every side, fog all morning. At about two in the afternoon the fog lifts, and the whole crew sees something.

[[Walton]] We perceived a low carriage, fixed on a sledge and drawn by dogs, pass on towards the north, at the distance of half a mile; a being which had the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic stature, sat in the sledge and guided the dogs. We watched the rapid progress of the traveller with our telescopes until he was lost among the distant inequalities of the ice.

That night the ice breaks up. And in the morning, the sailors are leaning over the side of the ship, talking to someone in the sea. It's a second sledge, drifted to them on a slab of ice. One dog alive. And a man inside it, nearly frozen, who will not come aboard until they tell him where the ship is going.

That's Victor Frankenstein. They carry him to the kitchen stove, rub him with brandy, and wrap him in blankets. Walton says it was two days before he could speak.

And here's the thing I keep coming back to. The crew saw the giant on the thirty-first of July. Victor came aboard on the first of August. Walton wrote it all down four days later, two weeks before Victor began his story, and Victor never corrected a word of that letter. It's Walton's hand and Walton's ink, all the way through. Whatever the crew saw that afternoon, a whole ship's company saw it, before any of them had heard his name.

I put that to Dr Kessler. She didn't try to get round it.

[[Walton]] Such a man has a double existence: he may suffer misery and be overwhelmed by disappointments, yet when he has retired into himself, he will be like a celestial spirit that has a halo around him, within whose circle no grief or folly ventures.

A double existence. When I first read that line, last year, it went straight past me. It's a compliment. Walton means a man with a rich inner world, who can go somewhere inside himself where grief can't reach.

Dr Kessler was very careful about it, and I want to be careful too.

[[Kessler]] He doesn't mean what we'd mean. I want to say that clearly, because it would be easy to make a cheap point. Walton is being poetic. But I'll tell you why I stopped there.
