# Episode 2: The Fever

[grave] [[Victor]] He can tell. Oh, save me! Save me! I imagined that the monster seized me; I struggled furiously and fell down in a fit.

That's Victor Frankenstein, a student in Ingolstadt, on the morning after the night he says he gave life to a body. His best friend has just arrived from Geneva. They're sitting down to breakfast. And Victor has seen something come into the room that his friend can't see.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance, season two. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, a psychiatrist named Marian Kessler asked me to read a set of old letters again, as if the dying man on the ice was the only one there. I said I'd give the idea a fair hearing. I also told you about the first page, the giant a whole ship's crew saw before Victor came aboard. That's still on my desk.

This week, Ingolstadt. Where it started.

---

Ingolstadt is a town on the Danube, in Bavaria. In Victor's day it had a university, and Victor arrived there at seventeen, just after his mother died. The university isn't in Ingolstadt any more. It moved, a long time ago, and its old records went with it. So I didn't go to Ingolstadt. I went to Munich, to a university archive, to a long table under a window, with a woman in grey gloves bringing me boxes.

Last year, in that same room, I found Victor's name in the register of students. Victor Frankenstein, of Geneva, student of natural philosophy. This time I was after something smaller. Victor says that after the November night he was ill for months. He couldn't have been at lectures. In a university that kept careful records, somebody might have noticed a student who stopped coming.

It took two days. Professors in those years kept notes on their students, lists of who attended and who didn't, sometimes with a remark beside the name. On the second afternoon, the archivist put a thin bundle in front of me and pointed to a line with her gloved finger.

[[Archive]] The student Frankenstein, of Geneva, has kept to his room this winter, ill of a fever, and has not attended.

No date I'm allowed to say, and no diagnosis. Just a professor, writing down that a young man from Geneva had disappeared into his room for a winter. I photographed it, and I sent the picture to Dr Kessler that night.

[[Kessler]] Ill of a fever. That covers a great deal in that century. It could be an infection. It could be exhaustion. It could be what we'd call a breakdown. The professor didn't know, and neither do we. But it tells us that something happened that winter that people outside Victor's head could see.

---

To understand the winter, you need the two years before it.

Victor tells Walton that at Ingolstadt he fell under the spell of chemistry, and then of one question: where does life come from? He studied anatomy. He watched bodies decay. And he says that from the middle of all that darkness, a light broke in on him. He had found out how to give life to dead matter.

That's the claim I can't test, and I said so last year. What I can test is what he did next, because he tells us. He spent nearly two years building a body. He worked alone, in what he calls a solitary chamber, "or rather cell, at the top of the house". He says that he "collected bones from charnel-houses". And then this.

[[Victor]] The dissecting room and the slaughter-house furnished many of my materials; and often did my human nature turn with loathing from my occupation, whilst, still urged on by an eagerness which perpetually increased, I brought my work near to a conclusion.

He describes himself in those months. Pale. Emaciated. Working at midnight by the moon. "My eyeballs were starting from their sockets." He stops writing home. A whole summer goes by, and he never sees it. He calls it a frantic impulse. Then he says something I'd underline twice if I could.

[[Victor]] I seemed to have lost all soul or sensation but for this one pursuit. It was indeed but a passing trance.

Dr Kessler had stopped there too.

[[Kessler]] Two years. Little sleep, little food, no friends, no letters. Working with the dead, alone, at night. He describes a trance. If I had to design conditions to break a mind, I couldn't do much better. I'm not saying that's what happened. I'm saying that if Victor had walked into my office after those two years and told me he'd seen a dead man stand up, my first question wouldn't be about chemistry.

---

[grave] Now the night itself. It was, Victor says, "a dreary night of November". One in the morning. Rain on the window. A candle nearly burned out. And by its last light, he says, he saw "the dull yellow eye of the creature open". It breathed. A convulsive motion went through its limbs.

He'd built it to be beautiful, he says, and it was the most horrible thing he'd ever seen. He ran out of the room. He walked up and down his bedroom for a long time, and finally lay down in his clothes. He dreamed of Elizabeth, the girl he grew up with, walking in the streets of Ingolstadt. He kissed her, and in his arms she turned into the corpse of his dead mother.

And he woke, and something was there.

[grave] [[Victor]] I beheld the wretch, the miserable monster whom I had created. He held up the curtain of the bed; and his eyes, if eyes they may be called, were fixed on me. His jaws opened, and he muttered some inarticulate sounds, while a grin wrinkled his cheeks. He might have spoken, but I did not hear; one hand was stretched out, seemingly to detain me, but I escaped and rushed downstairs.

He spends the rest of the night in the courtyard of the house, walking up and down in the rain, listening for footsteps. At six the porter opens the gates, and he walks out into the streets, drenched, without any idea where he's going.

I asked Dr Kessler what she hears in that passage.

[[Kessler]] I hear a man coming out of a nightmare and finding the nightmare still in the room. That's a known experience. People wake from a vivid dream and see a figure at the bed, very clearly, for a few seconds. It's frightening and it's common, especially in people who are exhausted. I'm not saying that's all it was. But look at the order. A dream of a corpse. Then a waking, by moonlight. Then a figure at the bed that tries to speak, and he can't hear what it says.

He might have spoken, but I did not hear. Last year that line was just eerie. This year I can't stop hearing it as something else. A voice he couldn't make out.

---

Morning. Victor is walking the streets in the rain when a coach pulls up from Switzerland, and out of it steps Henry Clerval.

Henry is the friend Victor grew up with in Geneva. A merchant's son who loved poetry and languages, and who'd begged his father for years to let him go to university. His father has finally said yes, and Henry has come all this way to join Victor. He's delighted. He's full of news from home.

Victor walks him back to his rooms, and on the way he has a thought that makes him shiver. What if the creature is still in his apartment? He tells Henry to wait at the bottom of the stairs. He runs up alone, throws the door open, and finds nothing. The rooms are empty. He's so relieved that he claps his hands and runs back down.

Keep that picture. Henry, at the bottom of the stairs. Victor, alone at the top.

The servant brings breakfast. And Victor can't sit still. He jumps over the chairs. He claps. He laughs, and Henry is frightened by the laugh, which Victor himself calls "loud, unrestrained, heartless". Henry asks what on earth is the matter. And Victor puts his hands over his eyes, because he says he saw the thing glide into the room.

[grave] [[Victor]] He can tell. Oh, save me! Save me! I imagined that the monster seized me; I struggled furiously and fell down in a fit.

Here's what I believe about this part, and why. In this one scene, there's another person in the room. Henry Clerval was sitting right there, across the breakfast table. And Victor never says Henry saw anything come in. He doesn't even claim it. Victor's own word, about his own experience, is imagined. I imagined that the monster seized me.

[[Kessler]] He tells us himself. That's what's so striking. He's describing an hallucination, and he knows it's one, at least about that moment. And then he generalizes. Look what he says about the months that follow.

---

[grave] [[Victor]] This was the commencement of a nervous fever which confined me for several months. During all that time Henry was my only nurse.

Henry nursed him through the whole winter. And Henry made a decision. Victor's father was old, and the journey was long, and Henry knew how frightened Elizabeth would be. So he hid from the family how ill Victor really was. He wrote home cheerfully. He sat by the bed.

[[Victor]] The form of the monster on whom I had bestowed existence was for ever before my eyes, and I raved incessantly concerning him.

Victor says Henry at first took the raving for "the wanderings of my disturbed imagination". But Victor kept coming back to it, over and over, and Henry began to think something terrible really had happened.

The leaves fell. Victor didn't see them. When he could finally look out of the window again, the buds were opening. It was spring.

So here's what we have from that winter. One professor's note, from outside, that he was ill. And from inside, Victor's own account of a figure that only he could see, a fit at the breakfast table, and months of raving about a monster to the one friend who never saw it.

I asked Dr Kessler to be blunt with me.

[[Kessler]] I'll be fair, not blunt. One more thing should be said, because it matters. Most people who go through something like this never hurt anyone. People with serious mental illness are far more often the victims of violence than the ones who commit it. If this theory is right, Victor would be the rare exception, not the rule. I don't want anyone listening to come away thinking otherwise.

I asked her where the theory begins, then, if it begins anywhere.

[[Kessler]] Here. A man who has worked himself to the edge produces, in his mind, a being. He makes it hideous. He makes it gigantic. He gives it everything he hates about what he's done. And then, for a whole winter, he raves about it to the only person who's there. If that's where this begins, the creature is born in this room, at this table. Not in the laboratory.

---

Here's the thing that nags me. Something was taken.

Last year I read you a minute from the town council of Ingolstadt, from those same years. Paul Okafor, the forensic pathologist who's been helping me since season one, reminded me of it when I called him.

[[Okafor]] A vault and a charnel house disturbed over a winter, bones missing. The doer unknown. That's in the town's own book, not in Victor's story. So somebody was taking remains. Victor says it was him, and I've no reason to doubt that part. The question is what he did with them. A man can rob a vault and still build nothing that walks. Or he can build something. I can't tell you which from a town minute.

The theory doesn't need there to be no laboratory. It doesn't even need there to be no body on the table. It only needs nothing to have stood up. And the town minute can't tell us whether anything stood up.

But somebody went into those vaults, more than once, over a winter, with profane fingers, as Victor puts it. I've seen the page. That happened.

---

Victor recovered that spring. He went back to his studies. Henry studied languages. They went for walks. For nearly two years, by Victor's own count, there was nothing. No figure at the window. No glimpse in the street. Whatever had stood at his bed on that November night, he didn't see it again.

Dr Kessler pointed something out on our last call, which I'll leave you with.

[[Kessler]] Notice when it goes quiet. It goes quiet when he's well. When he's eating and sleeping and walking in the countryside with his friend. The creature doesn't come back until something terrible happens at home.

[grave] And then, one Sunday afternoon in May, Victor and Henry came back from a fortnight's walking tour in the countryside around Ingolstadt. There was a letter waiting from Victor's father in Geneva. Victor opened it. And the first thing he read was that his little brother was dead.
