Episode 4. The Workshop.

[pause 1.0]

[grave] [[Victor]] In a solitary chamber, or rather cell, at the top of the house, and separated from all the other apartments by a gallery and staircase, I kept my workshop of filthy creation.

[grave] [[Victor]] The dissecting room and the slaughter-house furnished many of my materials.

[pause 1.5]

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) Moritz was tried for the murder of William Frankenstein, convicted, and executed the next morning. Victor Frankenstein sat in that court and said nothing. Afterwards, in her cell, he called himself "the true murderer".

This episode is the one I've been dreading. Because to understand why Victor says he stayed silent, I have to tell you what he says he did. And what he says he did should not be possible. I've read it a dozen times, and I still don't quite know what to do with it.

So let me set out the ground rules before we start.

I can't test the science. Nobody can. Victor never told anyone how it was done. Walton asked him, more than once, and Walton writes that "on this point he was impenetrable". Victor got angry and asked him whether he was mad.

What I can test is everything around it. The places. The timeline. Who saw what. And the parts of the story that, if they're true, are ordinary crimes. And I'll tell you now: everything around it holds.

[pause 1.5]

Victor Frankenstein went to the university of [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/) when he was seventeen, just after his mother's death. [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/) is a town on the Danube, in Bavaria, and in those days it had a university.

As a boy he'd been obsessed with old books of alchemy. Cornelius Agrippa, Paracelsus. At [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/) he met two professors. The first, Monsieur [Krempe](/kɹˈɛmpə/), a short man with a gruff voice, laughed at him for wasting his time on that nonsense. The second, Monsieur Waldman, was kinder. In his first lecture, Victor says, Waldman told the students that the ancient teachers "promised impossibilities and performed nothing", but that the modern scientists, the chemists, had "indeed performed miracles".

That lecture changed Victor's life. He threw himself into chemistry and anatomy. Within two years he was making discoveries of his own, and he stopped going home. And he became fixed on one question: where does life come from?

To answer it, he says, he first had to study death.

[grave] [[Victor]] Now I was led to examine the cause and progress of this decay and forced to spend days and nights in vaults and charnel-houses.

[pause 1.5]

I want to stop on that sentence, because this is the first crime in the file that Victor admits to, and it's one we can understand without believing anything strange.

A charnel house is a building where bones are stored, usually bones dug up from crowded graves to make room. Vaults are burial vaults. Victor says he spent days and nights in them. He says he "collected bones from charnel-houses". He says the dissecting room and the slaughterhouse "furnished many" of his materials. And elsewhere he asks Walton who could imagine "the horrors of my secret toil as I dabbled among the unhallowed damps of the grave or tortured the living animal to animate the lifeless clay".

I asked Dr [Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/), our legal historian in Geneva, what a man doing that would have been risking.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/) is not Geneva, so I speak generally, of Europe in that century. Anatomists had a lawful supply of bodies, small, and usually from the gallows. Everything beyond that supply was taken. From graves, from vaults, from charnel houses. To take the dead from consecrated ground without authority was a crime everywhere, and a serious one. Families guarded new graves. In some towns there were riots. On the documents, your Victor describes a trade in stolen human remains, conducted alone, at night, for nearly two years. If anyone had found that room, he would have been arrested. I am fairly sure of that.

So before we get to the impossible part, we already have a young man who, by his own account, is committing a serious crime in secret, every night, in a locked room at the top of a lodging house. He tells nobody. He stops writing home. His father doesn't reproach him. He just asks, more carefully than before, what Victor is working on.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] I'll say one thing about this and then I'll stop. He says he made the being about eight feet tall, because working on small parts was too slow. What he's describing is assembling a body from many bodies and from animals. Nobody knows how to make that live. Nobody knew then and nobody knows now. I'm not going to speculate on how you'd do it, and frankly I'd rather your listeners didn't either. What I can tell you is that the materials he lists are exactly what a body thief could get. Bones, tissue from dissection rooms, animal parts from a slaughterhouse. The shopping list is real. It's the last step that isn't.

[pause 1.5]

Then comes the night. Victor dates it only by month.

[grave] [[Victor]] It was on a dreary night of November that I beheld the accomplishment of my toils. With an anxiety that almost amounted to agony, I collected the instruments of life around me, that I might infuse a spark of being into the lifeless thing that lay at my feet. It was already one in the morning; the rain pattered dismally against the panes, and my candle was nearly burnt out, when, by the glimmer of the half-extinguished light, I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open; it breathed hard, and a convulsive motion agitated its limbs.

Victor says he had chosen the features to be beautiful. And that when the eyes opened, it was the most horrible thing he'd ever seen. Yellow skin that barely covered the muscles and arteries. Watery eyes almost the same colour as their sockets. Straight black lips.

And here's what he did. He ran. He went into his bedroom, walked up and down, and finally threw himself on the bed in his clothes. He had a nightmare about Elizabeth turning into the corpse of his mother. He woke up, and in the moonlight he says he saw the creature standing at his bed, holding up the curtain, looking at him.

[grave] [[Victor]] His jaws opened, and he muttered some inarticulate sounds, while a grin wrinkled his cheeks. He might have spoken, but I did not hear; one hand was stretched out, seemingly to detain me, but I escaped and rushed downstairs.

[grave] Read that again in your head. A being, a few hours old, holds out a hand to the only person it knows. It makes sounds. And he runs away.

I'm going to come back to that hand in the next episode, when we hear the other side's account of that night.

[pause 1.5]

Victor spent the rest of the night in the courtyard of the house. In the morning, when the porter opened the gates, he walked the streets in the rain, afraid, he says, to go back to his room. And at an inn where the coach stopped, he ran into someone he knew.

Henry [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/). His friend from Geneva, a merchant's son who loved poetry and languages, had finally talked his father into letting him come to [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/) to study. He'd just stepped off the coach.

Remember Henry. He's important in this story, and you'll hear more of him than of almost anyone except Victor. For now, here's the scene.

Victor brought Henry back to his rooms, but made him wait at the bottom of the stairs. Victor went up alone and threw the door open. The apartment was empty. So was the bedroom. Whatever had been there was gone.

Victor came down laughing. Not normal laughing. Henry says, according to Victor, "Do not laugh in that manner. How ill you are!" Victor thought he saw the creature glide into the room, cried out, "Oh, save me! Save me!" and collapsed.

That was the start of what Victor calls a nervous fever. It lasted for several months. Henry nursed him the whole time, and kept the seriousness of it from Victor's family. Victor says that in his fever he kept talking about the monster, and Henry thought it was delirium.

I asked Dr [Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/) what a fever like that could have been.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] I can't diagnose a man from a story, and I won't. But I can tell you what I'd be thinking about if he walked into a hospital. Two years of little sleep, poor food, working with decaying tissue by hand, in a cold room, with no idea of infection. Any one of those can make you ill. Together, months of fever with confusion is not surprising at all. And people with high fevers do see things. Vividly. They remember them as real afterwards.

So does that explain the figure at the bedside?

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] It could explain a figure at a bedside. It doesn't explain anything that other people saw. That's the whole question with this man, isn't it? Which parts did only he see?

[pause 1.5]

So I want to be fair here, because this is the point where a reasonable listener says: I know what happened in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/).

A brilliant, isolated young man, grieving his mother, spends two years robbing graves and working alone at night in an attic. He makes himself ill. He has a breakdown on a November night. He sees something in the moonlight. His best friend arrives the next morning, finds no one in the rooms, and nurses him through months of fever in which he talks about a monster.

On that reading, there is no creature. There's a sick man and his hallucination. And when his little brother is murdered, nearly two years later, the sick man sees his hallucination again, alone, in a thunderstorm, at the scene.

Here's the thing. I wanted to buy that. It's the comfortable answer, and I went looking for it. And Victor seems to have known it. Remember what he said, the morning he came home to Geneva? He said he'd had a nervous fever "just at the time that I dated my creation, and which would give an air of delirium to a tale otherwise so utterly improbable". He's making the argument against himself, before anyone else can.

What that reading has to explain, though, is the thing you heard at the top of the first episode. A ship's crew watching a giant through telescopes on the Arctic ice. Those sailors had never met Victor Frankenstein. And they saw it the day before he arrived.

A hallucination doesn't drive a dog sledge past a whole crew. Every time I think the sick-man answer explains this file, something solid gets in the way. And from here on, the solid things only get harder to explain away.

[pause 1.5]

After the fever, Victor slowly recovered. [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/) stayed with him. Victor says he couldn't bear the sight of his instruments again, or even the words natural philosophy. Professor [Krempe](/kɹˈɛmpə/) and Professor Waldman praised him to his face, and it made him sick.

And he told nobody. Not [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/), not his father, not Elizabeth.

Then, in May, his father's letter arrived. William was dead. And, as Victor says on the night of the storm at [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/), nearly two years had passed since the creature was made. What had it been doing all that time? Where was it?

Victor didn't know. And here we reach the strangest thing in this whole file. About two months after [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/)'s execution, Victor went up into the mountains above [Chamounix](/ʃæmunˈi/), alone, to a glacier called the sea of ice. And he says the creature came across the ice to meet him. Not to kill him. To talk.

He says they went into a hut on the mountain, sat by a fire, and the creature told him its whole life, from that November night onwards. Victor listened for hours. And then Victor went home and, years later, told it all to Robert Walton, who wrote it down.

So the next episode is the suspect's statement. It's the longest single passage in the letters. It includes a confession to the murder of William Frankenstein, in some detail. It includes an account of how the miniature got into [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/)'s pocket. And it is the part of the manuscript where Judith's second ink, Victor's corrections, are the heaviest.

I'm going to read it to you. And then we're going to check it against the one record of this case that Victor never touched.
