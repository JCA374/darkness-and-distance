She came as a servant. But Elizabeth is careful to tell Victor that in Geneva, a servant "does not mean the same thing as a servant in France and England". Caroline grew attached to her, and gave her a better education than she'd planned to. And [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/), in Elizabeth's words, "almost adored her protectress". [grave] Then the story turns. One by one, Elizabeth writes, [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/)'s brothers and sister died. Her mother began to think it was a judgement from heaven for loving the others more. [grave] A girl whose own mother blamed her for deaths she didn't cause. Remember that, because it's about to happen again, in a court, in front of thousands of people.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] I have found a file. I must be careful how I say this. It is a thin file, a few sheets, in a box with others from the same years, and the year on the box is damaged. The trial began at eleven o'clock. The family attended as witnesses. Victor went with them.

Victor says [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) was dressed in mourning and appeared calm. He says thousands were watching and that the crowd hated her. The advocate against her stated the charge, and witnesses were called.

First: she had been out the whole night of the murder. Towards morning, a market-woman had seen her not far from the spot where William's body was later found. The woman asked her what she was doing there. [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) gave her "a confused and unintelligible answer".

Second: she came home at about eight. When someone asked where she'd been, she said she'd been looking for the child, and asked if there was any news. When she was shown the body, she fell into violent hysterics and stayed in bed for several days.

Third, the picture. During those days in bed, a servant examined the clothes she'd worn that night and found the miniature in her pocket. That servant showed another servant, who went straight to a magistrate without telling the family.

And then Elizabeth was asked to identify it. She did. It was the same miniature she had placed round William's neck, an hour before he went missing. Victor says a murmur of horror went through the court.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] You must understand, in the procedure of the day, this is very serious. There is no witness to the killing. There is no confession, not yet. So the court is building from circumstances. She was near the place. She was out all night. She was confused. And she has the dead child's jewel in her clothing. For a court of that time, the jewel is almost everything. It connects her hand to his body.

[pause 1.5]

Then [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) spoke in her own defence. And this is the part I most want you to hear, because it's the first time in this whole file that we hear from her directly. Here's how she began.

"God knows," she said, "how entirely I am innocent. But I do not pretend that my protestations should acquit me; I rest my innocence on a plain and simple explanation of the facts which have been adduced against me."

Then she gave her account. With Elizabeth's permission, she'd spent that evening at the house of an aunt in [Chêne](/ʃˈɛn/), a village about a league from Geneva. Coming back at about nine, she met a man who asked if she'd seen anything of a child who was lost. She was frightened, and she spent several hours looking for him. By then the gates of Geneva were shut. She didn't want to wake the people in the nearby cottage, who knew her, so she spent the night in their barn.

She stayed awake most of the night. Towards morning she thinks she slept for a few minutes. Some steps disturbed her, and she woke. It was dawn. She left to keep looking for William. If she went near the place where his body lay, she didn't know it. And she was confused when the market-woman spoke to her because she had been up all night, and still didn't know what had happened to him.

About the picture, she could say nothing. And she said so, very plainly. "Did the murderer place it there? I know of no opportunity afforded him for so doing; or, if I had, why should he have stolen the jewel, to part with it again so soon?"

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] This is in the file also, in a shorter form. The clerk writes that she slept in a barn, that she was woken by steps, and that she cannot account for the portrait. Steps. Then she asked for character witnesses. Several people who had known her for years were called. They spoke well of her, but Victor says they were frightened, "timorous and unwilling to come forward". The crime was so hated that defending her felt dangerous.

So Elizabeth stood up. She asked permission to address the court. She said she was the dead child's cousin, or really his sister, and that it might seem improper for her to speak. But she wasn't going to watch someone die "through the cowardice of her pretended friends". She told the court how [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) had nursed Caroline in her last illness, and then her own mother. And then she went straight at the evidence.

She said, "as to the bauble on which the chief proof rests, if she had earnestly desired it, I should have willingly given it to her, so much do I esteem and value her."

I think that's the best argument anyone makes in this whole case. No motive. The owner of the jewel would have given it to her.

Victor says the court murmured with approval. But the approval was for Elizabeth's generosity. The anger in the room turned back on [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/), now for ingratitude as well.

And where was Victor? He says he couldn't stand it. When he saw that the crowd and the judges had made up their minds, he ran out of the court. He spent the night, in his words, in "unmingled wretchedness". In the morning he went back. And that's the line at the top of this episode. The ballots had been thrown, and they were all black.

The officer also told him something else. [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) had confessed.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] The confession is in the file. It is short. It is signed. It is dated after the verdict, not before. That is unusual, and I want to be precise: unusual, not impossible. What it tells me is that the court did not need it to condemn her. It needed it for something else. Perhaps to feel better.

The officer said as much to Victor. He said the judges don't like to condemn on circumstantial evidence, "be it ever so decisive", and he was glad of the confession.

A girl whose mother's confessor had told her mother that her children's deaths were a judgement. And now a confessor in a cell, telling her she was a murderer until she signed.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] On the documents, I cannot tell you what the confessor said. The file does not record it. I can tell you that the pressure she describes was real pressure, in that world. To die excommunicated was not a small thing. People confessed to many things for that.

So here's what we know. [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) Moritz, about whom every person who knew her well spoke kindly, was convicted of the murder of William Frankenstein on circumstantial evidence. The jewel in her pocket was the core of it. She confessed after the verdict, under threat of excommunication, and took the confession back the same day to the people who visited her. So, is that enough to clear her name? Not yet. A court convicted her on the jewel, and so far all we have is a girl who said she didn't do it and a clerk who wrote down that she heard steps. I believe her. Elizabeth believed her. The people who knew her spoke well of her, and were too afraid to say more. Here's what we don't know. Who put the miniature in her pocket. Who walked past that barn at dawn. And why the one person who says he knew the truth sat in that courtroom and said nothing.
