Episode 2. Plainpalais.

[pause 1.0]

Log. Geneva. A Thursday evening in May, a little after eight. I'm standing on the plain of [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/). It's a huge open square now, gravel and trees, right in the middle of the city. There are children everywhere. A little boy just ran past me and hid behind a tree, and his big brother is pretending he can't find him. I picked this evening on purpose. More than two hundred years ago, on a Thursday evening in May, a family from this city came here for a walk. They went home without their youngest son.

[grave] [[Victor]] About five in the morning I discovered my lovely boy, whom the night before I had seen blooming and active in health, stretched on the grass livid and motionless; the print of the murder's finger was on his neck.

That's a father writing. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) Frankenstein, to his son Victor, five days later. You're hearing it in Victor's reader's voice, because that's how it reached us. Victor recited the letter to Robert Walton on a ship in the ice, and Walton wrote it down.

[pause 1.5]

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, a captain in the Arctic pulled a dying man off the ice, the day after his crew saw a giant on a sledge. The man said his name was Victor Frankenstein, and that the one he was chasing was someone he had made.

This week I went to Geneva, to find the first name on his list of the dead. This is my log from that trip. Three days, three places, and one little boy.

[pause 1.5]

Day one. Wednesday. The state archives.

The archives are in the old town, at the top of the hill, in a building with stone stairs worn into dips by three hundred years of feet. Dr Anselm [Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/) met me at the door. He's a legal historian here, and you're going to hear a lot from him this season. On the phone, I'd asked him whether Geneva kept a record of its dead. He laughed at me, politely.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] Geneva keeps a record of everything. It is the most careful city in Europe about paper. Every parish has its register of burials. If a child of a syndic was buried in this city, he is upstairs. We only have to find the page.

It took us the whole morning. Big leather volumes, one parish after another, brown ink, French. [Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/) turned the pages with a flat wooden stick so his fingers never touched them. A little before lunch he stopped, and put the stick down, and slid the book across to me.

[[Archive]] William, son of Monsieur [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) Frankenstein, formerly syndic of this Republic. Found dead on the plain of [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/). Buried.

[[[Reber](/ɹˈAbəɹ/)]] There is a date, in May, a few days after the seventh. And there is the year, which I am told I must not say. On the documents, this is your boy.

I knew his name would be there. I still had to sit back for a minute. Until that morning, William Frankenstein was a line in a letter copied out by a sea captain at the far end of the world. Now he was in the city's own book, in a building Walton never entered.

So that evening, in my hotel, I went back to the letters, to find out who he was.

[pause 1.5]

Most of what we know about William in life comes from a letter his cousin Elizabeth wrote to Victor, in the March before he died. Victor recited this one for Walton too. And to read it, you need to know who was in that house.

The Frankensteins were one of the leading families of Geneva. Victor's father, [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/), had served as a syndic, one of the city's chief magistrates. Victor's mother, Caroline, had died years before. When Victor was seventeen, a girl in the house caught scarlet fever. Caroline was warned to stay away from the sickroom. She didn't. The girl recovered. Caroline caught the fever, and died.

The girl was Elizabeth [Lavenza](/ləvˈɛnzə/). She wasn't born a Frankenstein. When Victor was small, the family met her on a trip to the Lake of Como, an orphan living with poor foster parents, and brought her home to Geneva. The children called each other cousin, and Victor calls her "my more than sister". But there was no blood between them. On her deathbed, Victor says, his mother joined their hands and told them she'd always hoped they would marry. Shortly afterwards Victor left for the university of [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), in Bavaria, and he did not come home for nearly six years.

So by that spring, the house held [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/), Elizabeth, the two younger boys, Ernest and William, and one more person. A young woman named [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) Moritz, who came to live with the family at twelve, and had just come back to them. Remember her name. She's going to need her own episode.

And then there's William. Elizabeth describes him for Victor. He's tall for his age, she says. "Sweet laughing blue eyes, dark eyelashes, and curling hair." When he smiles, he has dimples. He already has one or two little wives, she writes, but his favourite is a girl called Louisa Biron, who is five.

I read that in a hotel room with the burial register still open on my laptop. A boy whose favourite girl was five years old. That's who this is about.

[pause 1.5]

Day two. Thursday. Back to the plain, and back to where I started this episode.

I'd saved the father's letter for this. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) wrote it on the twelfth of May, and I read it standing on the plain, while the light went.

It was a Thursday, he says, the seventh of May. A warm, calm evening. He, Elizabeth and the two boys came out here to walk. In those days [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/) was outside the city walls, a broad public walk with trees and paths, and room for children to run. The walls are gone now. The room to run is still here.

They stayed out later than usual. It was getting dark when they turned back, and William and Ernest, who'd gone on ahead, couldn't be found. The grown-ups sat down on a bench to wait. Ernest came back alone. He'd been playing with William, he said. William ran off to hide. Ernest looked for him and couldn't find him, and waited a long time, and William never came back.

[grave] They searched until night. Elizabeth thought he might have gone home on his own. He hadn't. They came back out with torches. And at about five in the morning, [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) found his son, lying on the grass.

I stood there until the boy behind the tree had been found, and gone home with his brother. Then I read the next part.

[grave] They carried William home. Elizabeth insisted on seeing him. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) tried to stop her. She went in, she looked at the boy's neck, and she cried out, and [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) quotes her: "O God! I have murdered my darling child!"

[grave] She fainted. When she came round, she explained. That evening William had begged her to let him wear something precious, a little portrait, a miniature, of his dead mother, Caroline. Elizabeth let him. And now the miniature was gone.

The family's conclusion was immediate, and very human. Somebody saw a jewel on a child, and killed him for it. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) writes that the picture "was doubtless the temptation which urged the murderer to the deed". Elizabeth blamed herself.

The letter ends with [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) asking Victor to come home, and asking him to come a certain way.

[grave] [[Victor]] Come, Victor; not brooding thoughts of vengeance against the assassin, but with feelings of peace and gentleness, that will heal, instead of festering, the wounds of our minds.

I called Judith Saville from a bench on the plain and read her that line. She was quiet for a moment.

[[Judith]] That's a father who knows his son, isn't it? He's not worried about the murderer. He's worried about Victor. And Robert copied it out in full, every line, the night Victor told it to him. Mind you, I think he knew it mattered.

[pause 1.5]

That night I had one more call to make. Dr Paul [Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/) is a forensic pathologist, retired now. Before the trip I'd sent him every passage in the letters that describes a death, and I asked him to start with William.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] I'll say first what I can't do. I can't examine anybody. I've got a father's letter, copied twice since. So everything I say is about what the words are consistent with. Nothing more.

I asked him what "the print of the murder's finger" means to him.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] It means pressure by a hand. When you strangle someone manually, the fingertips leave bruises, often small and oval, sometimes in a row. A father in that century would see those and say, a finger. That's fair. With a small child, it doesn't take a large hand, and it doesn't take long. I'm sorry to be blunt about that. What the body tells you is that a person put their hands on his throat. It doesn't tell you whose.

[grave] The mark of fingers on the neck. Hold onto that phrase. It isn't the last time we'll hear it.

[pause 1.5]

Day three. Friday night. [Secheron](/sˈɛʃəɹɑn/).

[Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/)'s letter found Victor in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/). He'd just come back from a fortnight's walking tour of the countryside there, with his friend Henry [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/), and the letter was waiting for him. He left for Geneva at once.

He says he travelled slowly, and stopped two days at Lausanne. By the time he reached Geneva it was completely dark, and the city gates were shut. So he spent the night at [Secheron](/sˈɛʃəɹɑn/), a village outside the walls. [Secheron](/sˈɛʃəɹɑn/) isn't a village any more. It's a part of the city, by the lake, with hotels and a tram stop. That's where I started, at about eleven at night, with Victor's statement open on my phone.

He says he couldn't rest, and decided to visit the spot where William died. He crossed the lake by boat. I walked it, along the water, about forty minutes. On his way, a storm came in over the mountains. On mine, there was a little rain, and a lot of traffic.

But he describes that storm like nothing else in his whole statement. Lightning playing on Mont Blanc. Thunder echoing off the mountains on every side. The lake lit up, he says, "like a vast sheet of fire". He climbs a low hill to watch it. The rain starts in large drops, and gets heavier, and he walks on through it, alone, towards the place where his brother was found. And he says that out loud, to the storm, he calls out: "William, dear angel! this is thy funeral, this thy dirge!"

I got to the plain a little before midnight. It was empty. I stood more or less where I'd stood the evening before, and I looked south. You can see it from there. A long grey wall of rock above the rooftops, the [Salêve](/səlˈɛv/). On a clear night it's just a mountain. Then I read what Victor says happened next.

[grave] [[Victor]] As I said these words, I perceived in the gloom a figure which stole from behind a clump of trees near me; I stood fixed, gazing intently: I could not be mistaken. A flash of lightning illuminated the object, and discovered its shape plainly to me; its gigantic stature, and the deformity of its aspect more hideous than belongs to humanity, instantly informed me that it was the wretch, the filthy daemon, to whom I had given life.

The figure passes him in the dark. Victor says that the moment the thought came to him, that this was his brother's murderer, he was sure of it. And then another flash, and he sees it again, already on the mountain.

[grave] [[Victor]] Another flash discovered him to me hanging among the rocks of the nearly perpendicular ascent of Mont [Salêve](/səlˈɛv/), a hill that bounds [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/) on the south. He soon reached the summit, and disappeared.

I've been up the [Salêve](/səlˈɛv/). There's a cable car now, and even that takes a while. I stood on that empty plain at midnight and looked at that wall of rock and tried to picture anything climbing it in a thunderstorm, in the time between two flashes of lightning.

Here's the thing that nags me. Only Victor saw that figure. One man, alone, in a storm, at the place his brother died. If that were all we had, I'd put it down to grief and lightning.

But it isn't all we have. Go back to the Arctic, to the last day of July, to a whole ship's crew looking through telescopes. Walton wrote down what they saw before Victor had said a single word about Geneva. "A being which had the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic stature." Two sightings, years apart and a thousand miles apart, by people who never met. And they describe the same size.

[grave] Victor stayed out on the plain all night, in the rain. At five in the morning the gates opened, and he walked into his father's house. And he told no one what he'd seen.

Because when his brother Ernest came down that morning, he had news. The family had already found William's murderer. It wasn't a giant. The missing miniature had turned up, in the pocket of someone who lived in that house.
