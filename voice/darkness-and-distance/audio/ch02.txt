[[Victor]] About five in the morning I discovered my lovely boy, whom the night before I had seen blooming and active in health, stretched on the grass livid and motionless; the print of the murder's finger was on his neck.

Those aren't Victor Frankenstein's words, even though you're hearing them in his reader's voice. They're his father's. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) Frankenstein wrote them in a letter to Victor, dated the twelfth of May. Years later, Victor recited it to Robert Walton on a ship in the ice. Walton wrote it down. So this is a father's letter, as remembered by his son, as copied by a stranger. When Victor quotes a letter at length, his reader voices it, because that's how it reached Walton: in Victor's mouth.

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, we met Robert Walton, an English captain who rescued a man from the Arctic ice and wrote down his story in letters to his sister. That man was Victor Frankenstein, of Geneva. This week, the first death.

[pause 1.5]

Before I tell you how William Frankenstein died, I want to tell you who was in that house.

The Frankensteins were one of the leading families of Geneva. Victor's father, [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/), had served as a syndic, one of the city's chief magistrates. That will matter later.

Victor's mother was Caroline. She'd been born Caroline Beaufort, the daughter of a merchant who lost everything. When Victor was a child, the family brought home a little girl they'd met on a trip to the Lake of Como, living with a poor family of foster parents. According to Victor, she was the orphaned daughter of a Milanese nobleman whose property had been confiscated. Her name was Elizabeth [Lavenza](/ləvˈɛnzə/). She grew up in the house, and the children called each other cousin. Victor describes her as "my more than sister".

Then there were the two younger boys. Ernest, the middle one. And William, the youngest.

When Victor was seventeen, Elizabeth caught scarlet fever. Caroline was warned to stay away from the sickroom. She didn't. Elizabeth recovered. Caroline caught the fever on the third day and died. On her deathbed, Victor says, she joined his hand with Elizabeth's and told them she'd always hoped they would marry. Shortly afterwards Victor left for the university of [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), in Bavaria. He did not come home again for nearly six years.

And there's one more member of the household, who isn't family. Her name is [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) Moritz. She's going to need her own episode, so for now I'll just say that she came to live with the Frankensteins when she was twelve, and that by the spring we're talking about, she had just come back to them.

Most of what we know about William in life comes from a letter Elizabeth wrote to Victor in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/), dated the eighteenth of March. Victor recited this one for Walton too.

He's tall for his age, she says. "Sweet laughing blue eyes, dark eyelashes, and curling hair." When he smiles, he has dimples. He already has, she writes, one or two little wives, but his favourite is a girl named Louisa Biron, who is five.

That's William. That's who we're talking about. Not a case number. A boy whose favourite girl, Louisa, was five years old.

[pause 1.5]

Here's what happened, from [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/)'s letter.

It was a Thursday, the seventh of May. A warm, calm evening. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/), Elizabeth and the two boys went to walk in [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/). That's a broad open plain just outside the city walls of Geneva, a public walk in those days, with trees and paths and space for children to run.

They stayed out later than usual. It was getting dark when they turned back, and William and Ernest, who'd gone on ahead, couldn't be found. The adults sat down to wait. Ernest came back alone. He'd been playing with William, he said. William had run off to hide, Ernest had looked for him and couldn't find him, and he'd waited a long time, and William never came.

They searched until night. Elizabeth suggested he might have gone home on his own. He hadn't. They went back out with torches. And at about five in the morning, [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) found his son lying on the grass.

Then comes a detail I want to hold onto. They carried William home. Elizabeth insisted on seeing him. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) tried to stop her. She went in, looked at the boy's neck, and she said, and [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) quotes her: "O God! I have murdered my darling child!"

She fainted. When she recovered, she explained. That evening, William had begged her to let him wear something precious: a small portrait, a miniature, of his dead mother, Caroline. Elizabeth let him. And now the miniature was gone.

The family's conclusion was immediate and very human. Somebody saw a valuable jewel on a child. Somebody killed him for it. [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/) writes that the picture "was doubtless the temptation which urged the murderer to the deed". Elizabeth blamed herself.

[pause 1.5]

I don't trust my own reading of a two-hundred-year-old description of a body. So I asked someone who reads bodies for a living. Dr Paul [Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/) is a forensic pathologist, retired now. I sent him every passage in the file that describes a death.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] I want to say first what I can't do. I can't examine anybody. I've got a father's letter, written days after he found his son, and copied twice since. So everything I say is about what the words are consistent with. Nothing more.

I asked him what "the print of the murder's finger" on a child's neck means to him.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] It means pressure by a hand. When you strangle someone manually, the fingertips leave bruises, often small and oval, sometimes in a row. A father in that century would see those and say, a finger. That's fair. What it doesn't tell you is how big the hand was, or whose. With a small child, it doesn't take a large hand, and it doesn't take long. I'm sorry to be blunt about that.

He also pointed out the word "livid".

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] Livid is a word people used for discolouration. Blue, grey, bruised. In strangulation, the face can be congested and dark. Again, consistent. What the body tells you is that a person put their hands on his throat. It doesn't tell you a story about why.

That's going to come up again. Hold onto the phrase: the mark of fingers on the neck. It isn't the last time we'll hear it.

I also asked him about time. William went missing at dusk and was found at five the next morning.

[[[Okafor](/Okˈɑfɔɹ/)]] People always want the time of death. From this, I can't give it to you. He could have died within minutes of running off to hide, or hours later. A cool spring night on open grass, no temperature taken, no examination recorded. The honest answer is somewhere in those ten or eleven hours, and anyone who tells you more precisely is guessing.

[pause 1.5]

There's one more passage in [Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/)'s letter that I keep going back to. It's the end. After he's told Victor everything, he asks him to come home, and he asks him to come home a certain way.

[[Victor]] Come, Victor; not brooding thoughts of vengeance against the assassin, but with feelings of peace and gentleness, that will heal, instead of festering, the wounds of our minds.

When I read that to Judith, she went quiet for a moment.

[[Judith]] That's a father who knows his son, isn't it? He's not worried about the murderer. He's worried about Victor. And Robert copied it out in full, every line, the night Victor told it to him. There are two little corrections on that page in the second ink, both place names. Victor fixing Robert's spelling of [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/), I think. Nothing else.

So keep the motive the family settled on in mind, because it's about to fall apart. If somebody killed a child for a jewel, you'd expect him to sell the jewel, or hide it, or throw it in the lake. You would not expect it to turn up, a few days later, in the pocket of someone who lived in the house.

[pause 1.5]

[Alphonse](/ˈælfɑns/)'s letter reached Victor in [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/). And here's something worth knowing. In early May, Victor and his friend Henry [Clerval](/klɛɹvˈɑl/), who'd come out to study there, had spent a fortnight on a walking tour of the countryside around [Ingolstadt](/ˈɪŋɡəlʃtæt/). They got back on a Sunday afternoon, and the letter was waiting. So on the seventh of May, by Victor's account, he was hundreds of miles from Geneva, with Henry. That's Victor's word, not a court's. But it fits the dates of the letter. Victor left at once for Geneva.

Now, this next part rests entirely on Victor's word. I want to be very clear about that, because this is the moment when his story stops being a family tragedy and becomes something else.

He says he travelled slowly. He stopped two days at Lausanne. By the time he reached Geneva it was dark, and the city gates were shut. So he spent the night at [Secheron](/sˈɛʃəɹɑn/), a village half a league outside the walls. He couldn't sleep, and he decided to visit the spot where William had died. He crossed the lake by boat to [Plainpalais](/plænpəlˈA/). On the way, a thunderstorm broke over the mountains. He walked through it. And then this.

[[Victor]] As I said these words, I perceived in the gloom a figure which stole from behind a clump of trees near me; I stood fixed, gazing intently: I could not be mistaken. A flash of lightning illuminated the object, and discovered its shape plainly to me; its gigantic stature, and the deformity of its aspect more hideous than belongs to humanity, instantly informed me that it was the wretch, the filthy daemon, to whom I had given life.

Victor says he saw this figure climb the nearly vertical face of Mont [Salêve](/səlˈɛv/), the mountain to the south, by the light of another flash, and disappear over the top.

I'll be honest about how this lands for me. A man alone at night, in a storm, at the scene of his brother's murder. He says he recognised, in a lightning flash, a being he believes he made. And he says in the very next breath, in his own words: "No sooner did that idea cross my imagination, than I became convinced of its truth." And later, "The mere presence of the idea was an irresistible proof of the fact."

That's not evidence. Victor seems to know it isn't. He says so himself.

[pause 1.5]

Because here's what he did next. Nothing.

He reached his father's house at about five in the morning. Before anyone was up, he'd already decided to keep quiet. And he explains why, very clearly. If someone else told him a story like this, he says, he'd have "looked upon it as the ravings of insanity". He reminds Walton that he'd had a nervous fever, a long one, at the very time he says this being was made. Nobody could catch a creature that climbs mountains in the rain. And so, in his words, "I resolved to remain silent."

I want to sit with that for a second. On the morning after he believes he has seen his brother's killer, Victor Frankenstein, the son of a magistrate, walks into his father's house and doesn't tell anyone.

His brother Ernest comes down first. He's grown up; Elizabeth's letter said he was sixteen. He tells Victor that their father is sinking under this, and that Elizabeth keeps blaming herself. But, Ernest says, there's one thing. Since the murderer has been discovered...

And Victor cuts him off. He says, how can that be? Who could pursue him? It's like trying to overtake the winds. "I saw him too; he was free last night!"

Ernest has no idea what he's talking about. Because the person the family has discovered is not a giant on a mountain.

[pause 1.5]

So, here's what we know about the murder of William Frankenstein.

He was a small boy, lost at dusk in a public park, found dead at five in the morning, with the marks of a hand on his neck. A valuable miniature he'd been wearing was gone. His family believed he'd been killed for it.

And here's what we don't know. Who did it. The only person in the file who claims to have seen the killer is Victor, alone, in a storm, at least a week after the event, and by his own account he told nobody.

What Ernest told him next is that, days earlier, one of the servants had gone through the clothes that [Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) Moritz had worn on the night of the murder. And in her pocket, the servant had found the miniature of Caroline Frankenstein.

[Justine](/ʤəstˈin/) was to be tried that same morning.
