# Episode 1: The Man on the Ice

[grave] [[Walton]] We perceived a low carriage, fixed on a sledge and drawn by dogs, pass on towards the north, at the distance of half a mile; a being which had the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic stature, sat in the sledge and guided the dogs. We watched the rapid progress of the traveller with our telescopes until he was lost among the distant inequalities of the ice.

That was written by a ship's captain, in the Arctic, in a letter to his sister. It is dated the fifth of August. The year is not given. Where the year should be, there's a seventeen, and then a dash.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

[grave] Here's what we know. One northern summer, more than two hundred years ago, an English ship was trapped in the ice somewhere north of Archangel. Its crew saw a giant drive a dog sledge across the frozen sea. The next morning they pulled a dying man off an ice floe. Over the following weeks that man told the captain a story, and the captain wrote it down. In that story there are five deaths. A little boy. A young woman, executed for the boy's murder. A young man found on an Irish beach. And two more, later on, that I'm not going to tell you about yet.

[grave] Only one person was ever convicted of any of it. Her name was Justine Moritz. She said she didn't do it.

[grave] The person the file names as the killer was never arrested, never charged and never found. He was last seen drifting north on a raft of ice.

For the last year, I have spent almost every working day checking a sea captain's letters against the record.

The more I learn, the stranger this case gets. And the more sources I find, the more of the letters turn out to be true.

Some of what these letters describe should be impossible. I know that. So I checked: dates, distances, who was where, who wrote what down and when. Then I went looking for records the captain never saw. A loft in Kent, the state archives in Geneva, a university register, the papers of the Paris police. Record after record, the letters were right.

[grave] There's one more reason I'm doing this. That young woman, Justine Moritz, is still written down in a register in Geneva as a murderer. Nobody has ever looked at it again. By the end of this season, I want to know if it's true.

So I'm going to work this like any cold case. I'll tell you where every piece comes from and who touched it. I'll tell you what made me believe it, and the things that still nag at me, because I'd rather you heard those from me. And underneath all of it is one question. Could it really be true?

---

It started with an email. It was polite, and it was long, and it had a spelling correction in a postscript.

The sender was a retired schoolteacher in Kent, in the south-east of England. Her name is Judith Saville. Her aunt had died the previous winter and left her a house full of furniture, most of which Judith gave away, and one sea chest, which she didn't.

[[Judith]] It's a sailor's chest, oak, with iron corners and a lid that weighs about as much as I do. My aunt kept blankets in it. Underneath the blankets, wrapped in oilcloth, there were letters. A great many letters, folded the old way, without envelopes, and every one of them addressed to the same person. Mrs Saville, England. That's all. Mrs Saville, England. As though the post would simply know.

Mrs Saville is Margaret Saville, and Judith is, as she puts it, a niece several times over.

[[Judith]] Great-great-great, and I lose count after that, mind you. The letters are from her brother, Robert Walton. Robert went to sea, and he wrote to her from everywhere he stopped. Saint Petersburg, first. Then Archangel. Then nowhere at all, just a date and the ice.

I asked Judith what made her write to a podcast, and not to a museum.

[[Judith]] I did write to a museum, years ago. They were very kind. They said it read like a novel. And it does, in places. But every time I read them, I hear a man writing home to his sister. I always hoped someone would come and find out how much of it was true. I think you might be that person.

No pressure, then. I'll admit something. I nearly wrote back the same thing the museum did. Instead I asked her one question. Is there anything else up there?

There was. A week later Judith went back up into her aunt's loft, behind the water tank, and came down with a cardboard box she'd never opened. Inside it, more family papers. And the letters themselves turned out to have something on the back that nobody had looked at properly. When a letter came, Margaret Saville folded it and wrote a note on the outside, the way people filed their post then. Judith read me the first two.

[[Archive]] Robert. From Saint Petersburgh. Received.

[[Archive]] Robert. From Archangel. Received.

[[Judith]] It's her hand. I've seen it in the family Bible, where she wrote the births. Small and upright, with a long tail on the R. These weren't kept as a curiosity. They were opened, and read, and put away by his sister, as they came.

That's the first thing I needed to know. These letters were real post. They crossed real seas and reached a real woman, who filed them. Whatever else is in them, somebody lived it.

Judith had one condition before she'd send me anything else. Every one of those papers has a year on it, somewhere. The letters don't, but the family papers do.

[[Judith]] I'd rather you didn't say it. Not on the air. Robert never put it in his letters, and if you say the year, I'll have men with metal detectors in the garden by Tuesday. I've had them before, over a Roman coin.

So I won't. Not for her papers, and not for any record I found after them. There was one more thing at the bottom of that box, a later family letter. Judith read it to herself, put it down, and said, not yet. You'll hear it in the last episode. Which brings us to the man who wrote the letters. Here's the first thing to know about Robert Walton. He was not a scientist and not a soldier. He grew up on his uncle's books of voyages, failed as a poet, inherited a fortune, and spent six years training for the sea, twice as an under-mate on a Greenland whaler. In his very first letter, from Saint Petersburg, in December, he tells his sister why he's going.

[[Walton]] I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never before imprinted by the foot of man.

He wanted to reach the North Pole. And he says he is lonely. From Archangel, in March, he complains to Margaret that he will "certainly find no friend on the wide ocean". Remember that line. It matters later, because he does find one.

---

The letters are Robert Walton's. They're dated, they're in his hand, and he wrote them as things happened. That's the first layer, and it's the best kind of evidence a story like this ever gets: a witness writing it down the same week.

But most of the pages in that chest are not Walton's story. Inside his letters, Walton copied out a very long statement. It was told to him aloud, over about a week, by the man his crew pulled off the ice. Walton says he wrote it up every night, "as nearly as possible in his own words". That's the second layer. It's a statement from a dying man, recorded by a sympathetic listener, after the fact.

And inside that statement there's a third layer. The dying man repeats, at length, what someone else told him, on a glacier in the Alps, years before. That someone is the person he accuses of murder.

So when you hear a voice reading in this series, ask which layer it comes from. Three actors read the documents for us: one for Walton's letters, one for the dying man, one for the words he says the suspect spoke. A fourth reads the records I found in the archives. They are performances, not recordings.

There's one more thing about the second layer. Judith showed it to me on a video call, holding a page up to her kitchen window.

[[Judith]] You see this? Robert's hand is very regular. He was taught well. But here, and here, in a darker ink, there are words scraped away and other words written over them. Different hand. Smaller, slanting the other way. And it's all through the middle section. Not every page, but most of them, and the heaviest where somebody else is speaking.

Walton explains it himself, near the end. The man he rescued found out that Walton was taking notes.

[[Walton]] Frankenstein discovered that I made notes concerning his history; he asked to see them and then himself corrected and augmented them in many places, but principally in giving the life and spirit to the conversations he held with his enemy.

So the man who tells the story edited the story. And he edited most heavily exactly the part where his enemy speaks. You should know that from the start, and I'll never hide it from you. But here's what surprised me. Again and again, where his statement can be laid next to something he never touched, a trial record, a register, Walton's own eyes, the two agree. Over this season I'm going to show you every one of those, in the order I found them.

---

Let's go to the ice.

By the end of July, Walton's ship is in the far north. On Monday, the thirty-first, the ice closes around them. There's thick fog. About two o'clock in the afternoon the fog lifts, and that's when the crew see what you heard at the top of this episode: a sledge, a team of dogs, and a figure guiding it, half a mile off, heading north.

I want to be precise about what this is. It is the only time in the entire file that a group of people sees the suspect and the account doesn't come to us through Victor. Walton writes "we perceived" and "we watched". They used telescopes. Then the figure was gone among the ridges of ice, and the ship was stuck, so nobody could follow.

That night the ice broke up. Walton waited for daylight. And in the morning he went on deck.

[grave] [[Walton]] In the morning, however, as soon as it was light, I went upon deck and found all the sailors busy on one side of the vessel, apparently talking to someone in the sea. It was, in fact, a sledge, like that we had seen before, which had drifted towards us in the night on a large fragment of ice. Only one dog remained alive; but there was a human being within it whom the sailors were persuading to enter the vessel.

This man was not a giant. Walton calls him "a European". He spoke English with a foreign accent. And before he would come aboard, half frozen, with one live dog left, he asked a question.

[[Walton]] "Before I come on board your vessel," said he, "will you have the kindness to inform me whither you are bound?"

Walton told him they were going north, to the pole. And only then did he agree to be saved.

[grave] I've read that passage maybe thirty times. A man on a shrinking piece of ice, refusing a rescue until he knows which direction it's going. I don't think he was asking to go home. I think he was asking whether the ship would carry him the way the giant had gone.

---

They brought him round with brandy and soup, beside the kitchen stove. For two days he couldn't speak. When he could, Walton kept the crew away from him. But once, the lieutenant asked him why he'd come so far onto the ice in a vehicle like that. Walton says his face went dark, and he answered:

[grave] [[Victor]] To seek one who fled from me.

The lieutenant asked if the man he was chasing travelled the same way. Yes. Then, the lieutenant said, we think we saw him, the day before we picked you up. And Walton writes that the stranger then asked "a multitude of questions" about the route the other one had taken, a creature he called the daemon. That's the old spelling of demon.

So, by the first week of August, Walton has two independent facts. His crew saw a gigantic figure on a sledge. And a man the crew rescued says he was chasing someone who travels by sledge. Two sources that never met, and they match. Neither one proves murder. But Walton didn't invent either of them, and he saw the first with his own eyes, through a telescope, with his whole crew beside him.

That's the first thing in this file that checks out. It won't be the last.

---

For the next three weeks, the letters change. They're no longer about the voyage. They're about the guest. Walton writes on the fifth of August, the thirteenth, the nineteenth. He calls the man gentle and wise and, more than once, noble. I asked Judith what she makes of that.

[[Judith]] Robert had found his friend, hadn't he? He tells Margaret he begins to love the man as a brother. You can feel him relax on the page, poor thing. The handwriting gets looser. He's not lonely any more.

That matters for us, because it means the man who wrote down the statement was not a neutral clerk. He was devoted to the man who was talking. Hold onto that. I'll come back to it at the end of this episode.

On the thirteenth of August, Walton is talking about his own ambitions. He says he'd give his fortune, his life, anything, for the knowledge he's after. And the guest breaks down in tears and says this:

[grave] [[Victor]] Unhappy man! Do you share my madness? Have you drunk also of the intoxicating draught? Hear me; let me reveal my tale, and you will dash the cup from your lips!

Six days later he offers to tell it. Walton copies down what he said, and the passage is long, so I'll give you the part that matters most.

[[Victor]] Prepare to hear of occurrences which are usually deemed marvellous. Were we among the tamer scenes of nature I might fear to encounter your unbelief, perhaps your ridicule; but many things will appear possible in these wild and mysterious regions which would provoke the laughter of those unacquainted with the ever-varied powers of nature; nor can I doubt but that my tale conveys in its series internal evidence of the truth of the events of which it is composed.

Internal evidence. That's a lawyer's phrase, almost. He's telling Walton: you won't be able to check most of this, but it hangs together, and that's the proof.

I've heard a lot of people say that. Usually they were wrong. This time I didn't have to take his word for it. I could check.

---

So here's where we are at the end of day one. The man on the ice gave his name. He was from Geneva, from a family of magistrates. His name was Victor Frankenstein.

And the first thing his statement says, once he gets past his childhood, is that the person he was chasing across the ice was not someone he'd met. It was someone he had made.

Could that really be true? Everything I was taught says no. So let me tell you plainly where I've ended up, and why, and then you can spend the season checking me.

I believe Robert Walton's letters. I have three reasons.

One. The crew saw the giant first. A whole ship, through telescopes, the day before anybody told them a story. Nobody planted that.

Two. Every record I found that the letters could be checked against, and there are a lot of them, agrees with the letters. I'll show you each one. Not one of them contradicts him.

Three. The letters keep the things a liar would cut. Victor's own failures. His silence. The moment, later on, when the people around him say they think he's imagining things. If you were inventing this story to be believed, you'd take those out. They're all still there.

And the thing that nags me? Walton loved this man. He says so. A man who loves you will write your story the way you tell it. I'll keep that in front of me the whole way. But Walton also saw what he saw, with his own eyes, before he'd heard a word. And before the end, he'd hold in his own hands letters that only make sense if the story is real.

[grave] And the first thing that holds is the worst. A little boy died in a park in Geneva one evening in May, and his father found him at five in the morning. Next time, we start there.

This has been Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.
