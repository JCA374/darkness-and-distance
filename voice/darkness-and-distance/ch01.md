# Episode 1: The Man on the Ice

[[Walton]] We perceived a low carriage, fixed on a sledge and drawn by dogs, pass on towards the north, at the distance of half a mile; a being which had the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic stature, sat in the sledge and guided the dogs. We watched the rapid progress of the traveller with our telescopes until he was lost among the distant inequalities of the ice.

That was written by a ship's captain, in the Arctic, in a letter to his sister. It is dated the fifth of August. The year is not given. Where the year should be, there's a seventeen, and then a dash.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

Here's what we know. One northern summer, more than two hundred years ago, an English ship was trapped in the ice somewhere north of Archangel. Its crew saw a giant drive a dog sledge across the frozen sea. The next morning they pulled a dying man off an ice floe. Over the following weeks that man told the captain a story, and the captain wrote it down. In that story there are five deaths. A little boy. A young woman, executed for the boy's murder. A young man found on an Irish beach. A bride on her wedding night. And an old man who, by his son's account, died of grief.

Only one person was ever convicted of any of it. Her name was Justine Moritz, and almost everyone who has read the file agrees she didn't do it.

The person the file names as the killer was never arrested, never charged and never found. He was last seen drifting north on a raft of ice.

This season, I'm going to go through that file the way I'd go through any cold case. I'm going to tell you where every piece of it comes from, who wrote it down, and who touched it afterwards. I'm not going to ask you to believe anything the documents can't carry. And I'll tell you now, because you'll hear it in my voice soon enough: some of what these documents describe is impossible. I know that. I'm going to report it anyway, because the people in this story died, and the impossible part is where the record says the answer is.

---

It started with an email. It was polite, and it was long, and it had a spelling correction in a postscript.

The sender was a retired schoolteacher in Kent, in the south-east of England. Her name is Judith Saville. Her aunt had died the previous winter and left her a house full of furniture, most of which Judith gave away, and one sea chest, which she didn't.

[[Judith]] It's a sailor's chest, oak, with iron corners and a lid that weighs about as much as I do. My aunt kept blankets in it. Underneath the blankets, wrapped in oilcloth, there were letters. A great many letters, folded the old way, without envelopes, and every one of them addressed to the same person. Mrs Saville, England. That's all. Mrs Saville, England. As though the post would simply know.

Mrs Saville is Margaret Saville. She married into Judith's family, and Judith is, as she puts it, a niece several times over.

[[Judith]] Great-great-great, and I lose count after that, mind you. The letters are from her brother, Robert Walton. Robert went to sea, and he wrote to her from everywhere he stopped. Saint Petersburg, first. Then Archangel. Then nowhere at all, just a date and the ice.

I asked Judith what made her write to a podcast, and not to a museum.

[[Judith]] I did write to a museum. They wrote back very kindly and said it was an interesting piece of fiction. I don't think it is fiction. I think it's a man writing to his sister. Whether everything he was told was true is another question, and that's your job, not mine.

That's fair. So here's the first thing to know about Robert Walton. He was not a scientist and not a soldier. He was a young man who grew up reading his uncle's books of voyages, tried and failed to be a poet, inherited a fortune from a cousin, and then spent six years training for the sea. He sailed with the whalers to the North Sea, and twice hired himself out as an under-mate on a Greenland whaler. In his very first letter, from Saint Petersburg, in December, he tells his sister why he's going.

[[Walton]] I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never before imprinted by the foot of man.

He wanted to reach the North Pole. He tells his sister he can't make himself picture it as frost and desolation; he imagines a calm sea and a land of wonders. And he says he is lonely. From Archangel, in March, he complains to Margaret that he will "certainly find no friend on the wide ocean". Remember that line. It matters later, because he does find one.

---

Before we go any further, I need to explain what we're actually listening to, because it's going to matter in every episode.

The letters are Robert Walton's. They're dated, they're in his hand, and he wrote them as things happened. That's the first layer, and it's the best kind of evidence a story like this ever gets: a witness writing it down the same week.

But most of the pages in that chest are not Walton's story. Inside his letters, Walton copied out a very long statement. It was told to him aloud, over about a week, by the man his crew pulled off the ice. Walton says he wrote it up every night, "as nearly as possible in his own words". That's the second layer. It's a statement from a dying man, recorded by a sympathetic listener, after the fact.

And inside that statement there's a third layer. The dying man repeats, at length, what someone else told him, on a glacier in the Alps, years before. That someone is the person he accuses of murder.

So when you hear a voice reading in this series, ask yourself which layer it comes from. I've asked three actors to read the documents for us. One reads Robert Walton's letters. One reads the dying man. And one reads the words the dying man says were spoken to him by the suspect. They are performances, not recordings. Nobody recorded anything in that century.

There's one more thing about the second layer. Judith showed it to me on a video call, holding a page up to her kitchen window.

[[Judith]] You see this? Robert's hand is very regular. He was taught well. But here, and here, in a darker ink, there are words crossed out and other words written above them. Different hand. Smaller, slanting the other way. And it's all through the middle section. Not every page, but most of them, and the heaviest where somebody else is speaking.

Walton explains it himself, near the end. The man he rescued found out that Walton was taking notes.

[[Walton]] Frankenstein discovered that I made notes concerning his history; he asked to see them and then himself corrected and augmented them in many places, but principally in giving the life and spirit to the conversations he held with his enemy.

So the man who tells the story edited the story. And he edited most heavily exactly the part where his enemy speaks. I'm not saying that makes him a liar. I'm saying that's the condition of the evidence, and you should know it from the start.

---

Let's go to the ice.

By the end of July, Walton's ship is in the far north. On Monday, the thirty-first, the ice closes around them. There's thick fog. About two o'clock in the afternoon the fog lifts, and that's when the crew see what you heard at the top of this episode: a sledge, a team of dogs, and a figure guiding it, half a mile off, heading north.

I want to be precise about what this is. It is the only time in the entire file that a group of people sees the suspect and the account doesn't come to us through Victor. Walton writes "we perceived" and "we watched". They used telescopes. Walton thought they were "many hundred miles from any land", and the sight suggested to him that they weren't. Then the figure was gone among the ridges of ice, and the ship was stuck, so nobody could follow.

That night the ice broke up. Walton waited for daylight. And in the morning he went on deck.

[[Walton]] In the morning, however, as soon as it was light, I went upon deck and found all the sailors busy on one side of the vessel, apparently talking to someone in the sea. It was, in fact, a sledge, like that we had seen before, which had drifted towards us in the night on a large fragment of ice. Only one dog remained alive; but there was a human being within it whom the sailors were persuading to enter the vessel.

This man was not a giant. Walton calls him "a European". He spoke English with a foreign accent. And before he would come aboard, half frozen, with one live dog left, he asked a question.

[[Walton]] "Before I come on board your vessel," said he, "will you have the kindness to inform me whither you are bound?"

Walton told him they were going north, to the pole. And only then did he agree to be saved.

I've read that passage maybe thirty times. A man on a shrinking piece of ice, refusing a rescue until he knows which direction it's going. I don't think he was asking to go home. I think he was asking whether the ship would carry him the way the giant had gone.

---

They brought him round with brandy and soup, beside the kitchen stove. For two days he couldn't speak. When he could, Walton kept the crew away from him. But once, the lieutenant asked him why he'd come so far onto the ice in a vehicle like that. Walton says his face went dark, and he answered:

[[Victor]] To seek one who fled from me.

The lieutenant asked if the man he was chasing travelled the same way. Yes. Then, the lieutenant said, we think we saw him, the day before we picked you up. And Walton writes that the stranger then asked "a multitude of questions" about the route the other one had taken, a creature he called the daemon. That's the old spelling of demon.

So, by the first week of August, Walton has two independent facts. His crew saw a gigantic figure on a sledge. And a man the crew rescued says he was chasing someone who travels by sledge. Neither fact proves anything about murder. But the second fits the first, and Walton didn't invent either one. He saw the first with his own eyes.

---

For the next three weeks, the letters change. They're no longer about the voyage. They're about the guest. Walton writes on the fifth of August, the thirteenth, the nineteenth. He calls the man gentle and wise and, more than once, noble. I asked Judith what she makes of that.

[[Judith]] Robert had found his friend, hadn't he? He tells Margaret he begins to love the man as a brother. You can feel him relax on the page, poor thing. The handwriting gets looser. He's not lonely any more.

That matters for us, because it means the man who wrote down the statement was not a neutral clerk. He was devoted to the man who was talking. He believed him, or wanted to. We should keep that in mind the way a jury would.

On the thirteenth of August, Walton is talking about his own ambitions. He says he'd give his fortune, his life, anything, for the knowledge he's after. And the guest breaks down in tears and says this:

[[Victor]] Unhappy man! Do you share my madness? Have you drunk also of the intoxicating draught? Hear me; let me reveal my tale, and you will dash the cup from your lips!

Six days later he offers to tell it. Walton copies down what he said, and the passage is long, so I'll give you the part that matters most.

[[Victor]] Prepare to hear of occurrences which are usually deemed marvellous. Were we among the tamer scenes of nature I might fear to encounter your unbelief, perhaps your ridicule; but many things will appear possible in these wild and mysterious regions which would provoke the laughter of those unacquainted with the ever-varied powers of nature; nor can I doubt but that my tale conveys in its series internal evidence of the truth of the events of which it is composed.

Internal evidence. That's a lawyer's phrase, almost. He's telling Walton: you won't be able to check most of this, but it hangs together, and that's the proof.

I've covered a lot of cases where someone said that. Sometimes they were right.

---

So here's where we are at the end of day one.

A captain's letters, written as things happened, survive in a sea chest in Kent. In them, the captain's crew sees a giant on the ice. The captain rescues a man who is hunting someone. And the man begins a statement that the captain writes down every night, and that the man himself later corrects, in a second ink.

The man gave his name. He was from Geneva, from a family of magistrates. His name was Victor Frankenstein.

And the first thing his statement says, once he gets past his childhood, is that the person he was chasing across the ice was not someone he'd met. It was someone he had made.

I don't believe that, by the way. Not yet. But I believe that a little boy died in a park in Geneva one evening in May, and that his father found him at five in the morning. Next time, we start there.

This has been Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.
