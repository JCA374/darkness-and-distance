# Episode 6: The Irish Shore

Here is a deposition given before a magistrate, on the coast of Ireland, by a fisherman who had tripped over something on a dark beach. It's in Victor's statement, in his own summary, so it reaches us third-hand. But it's the only description we have of how they found him.

[[Victor]] It appeared to be a handsome young man, about five and twenty years of age. He had apparently been strangled, for there was no sign of any violence except the black mark of fingers on his neck.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, we heard the suspect's statement: the creature's own account, as Victor relayed it, of killing William Frankenstein and hiding the miniature on Justine Moritz as she slept in a barn. A detail in it matches Justine's trial file, which Victor never touched. And at the end, Victor agreed to make the creature a companion.

This episode is about the man who was with Victor when he went to do it.

---

Henry Clerval was the son of a merchant of Geneva. As a boy, Victor says, he wrote heroic songs and tales of enchantment and tried to make the other children act out plays about King Arthur. He loved, in Victor's words, "enterprise, hardship, and even danger for its own sake".

You met him in episode four, stepping off a coach in Ingolstadt the morning after the November night. He'd fought for years to get his father's permission to study, and when he arrived he found his best friend raving. He nursed him for months. He studied Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit. His plan was to go to India, to help, as he saw it, with the progress of European trade and colonisation there. He was ambitious, cheerful and loyal, and he didn't know any of what you now know.

After the glacier, Victor needed a laboratory, and he needed to be away from Geneva. He talked his father into letting him travel to England, and Henry came too. They went down the Rhine, crossed to London, and stayed for months. Henry met people who could help with his Indian plans. Victor collected, as he puts it, the materials for his new creation. They left London on the twenty-seventh of March and toured: Windsor, Oxford, Matlock, the lakes of Cumberland, Edinburgh. They were invited to stay with a friend at Perth.

And in Scotland, Victor told Henry he wanted to make a tour alone. A month or two. Henry tried to talk him out of it, then gave in. Here's the last thing Victor records him saying to his face.

"I had rather be with you," he said, "in your solitary rambles, than with these Scotch people, whom I do not know; hasten, then, my dear friend, to return, that I may again feel myself somewhat at home, which I cannot do in your absence."

---

Victor went north, through the Highlands, and chose an island at the far end of the Orkneys. He describes it as hardly more than a rock: three miserable huts, five inhabitants, the mainland about five miles off. He rented the empty hut, had it repaired, and started work.

One night, by moonlight, he looked up from what he was doing, and the creature was at the window, watching him. Victor says it had followed him the whole way.

And Victor destroyed the female, there, in front of it.

[[Victor]] As I looked on him, his countenance expressed the utmost extent of malice and treachery. I thought with a sensation of madness on my promise of creating another like to him, and trembling with passion, tore to pieces the thing on which I was engaged.

The creature howled and left. Hours later it came back, into the hut, and they argued. Victor told it, "never will I create another like yourself." And the creature made a threat. It was long, and it ended like this.

[[Creature]] It is well. I go; but remember, I shall be with you on your wedding-night.

Then, Victor says, it got into its boat and crossed the water fast towards the mainland, and was gone.

I'll come back to that boat. I'll also come back to how Victor understood that threat. Because he understood it in a very particular way. He thought it meant the creature would kill him, Victor, on the night he married.

---

The next day, a fishing boat brought Victor a packet of letters. One was from Henry. His friends in London wanted him back to finish arrangements for India. He couldn't wait much longer. Would Victor please leave his island and meet him at Perth, so they could travel south together?

Victor decided to go. First he had to clear the hut. He packed the remains of the half-finished creature into a basket, with a great many stones. And between two and three in the morning, he sailed out about four miles in a small skiff, waited until a cloud covered the moon, and dropped the basket into the sea.

I asked Dr Reber about that.

[[Reber]] He is very careful to tell Walton he was not seen. He says he avoided other boats. In the law of any country, a man sinking remains at sea in the dark would be a man destroying evidence. Of what, here, is a philosophical question. But it is what he says he did.

Then Victor did something odd. He lay down in the bottom of the boat and fell asleep. When he woke, the sun was high, the wind was strong from the north-east, and he'd been driven a long way from his island. He had no compass with him and no idea where he was. He was out all day. And as the sun went down, he saw land, followed the coast to a small, neat town with a good harbour, and sailed in.

People crowded around him on the quay. They weren't friendly. He asked them, in English, where he was. A man told him, "it is the custom of the Irish to hate villains." Then someone took him by the shoulder and said he had to come to Mr Kirwin's, the magistrate, "to give an account of the death of a gentleman who was found murdered here last night".

---

Mr Kirwin, Victor says, was an old, kindly man. He called witnesses, and they gave depositions in front of Victor.

A fisherman said that the night before, about ten o'clock, he'd been coming home with his son and his brother-in-law, Daniel Nugent. It was very dark, no moon yet. They landed at a creek about two miles down the coast from the harbour. He was walking ahead along the sand with the tackle when he tripped over something and fell flat. His companions brought up the lantern. It was the body of a man.

They thought at first he'd drowned and washed up. But his clothes weren't wet. And he wasn't cold. They carried him to an old woman's cottage nearby and tried to bring him round, and Daniel ran to town for an apothecary, and it was too late.

That's the passage you heard at the top. About twenty-five. Strangled. No other sign of violence. The black mark of fingers on his neck.

Then Daniel Nugent spoke. He swore that just before his companion fell, he'd seen a boat with one man in it, a little way out from the shore. And as far as he could tell by starlight, it was the same boat Victor had just landed in. A woman who lived near the beach said that about an hour before she heard of the body, she'd seen a boat with one man in it push off from that part of the shore.

So now you're Mr Kirwin. A stranger lands the next evening, in a small boat, alone, with no good account of himself. Witnesses put a boat like his at the scene. And when the fisherman described the finger marks, the stranger had to grab a chair to stay standing.

Mr Kirwin did the obvious thing. He took Victor to see the body.

[[Victor]] I entered the room where the corpse lay and was led up to the coffin. How can I describe my sensations on beholding it?

It was Henry.

---

Victor threw himself on the body. He cried out, according to his own account, that his "murderous machinations" had killed Henry too. Then he collapsed. For two months he had a fever and was close to death. He says his ravings were "frightful", and this is the line I want you to hear.

[[Victor]] I called myself the murderer of William, of Justine, and of Clerval.

He was raving in his own language, so only Mr Kirwin understood. A man accused of a murder, calling himself a murderer, again and again, for two months.

When he came round, he was in prison. He'd been there, all told, three months, before the assizes. And his father came from Geneva to sit with him.

[[Reber]] Now, this is the part that is important for us. The case goes to a grand jury. In the British system, and Ireland used it, a grand jury does not decide guilt. It decides if there is a case to answer. If they find a true bill, there is a trial. If they reject the bill, there is no trial. Here, Victor says, the grand jury rejected the bill. Because it was proved that he was on the Orkney Islands at the hour the body was found. That is an alibi, accepted by strangers, in a country that had no reason to love him.

Kirwin collected the witnesses. Remember, there were five people on Victor's island, and Victor says he'd been talking to some of them about the time the body was found. And there's the timing, which took me a while. The fishermen found Henry at about ten o'clock at night. At that hour, Victor was still on his island. He didn't put out to sea with his basket until two or three the next morning, and he only reached Ireland the following evening. Within about a fortnight of the hearing, Victor was free. On the voyage home, he says, he took laudanum every night, to be able to sleep.

---

Now let me put this next to what we already have.

I asked Dr Okafor to read the two descriptions together. William, in the park in Geneva. Henry, on the sand in Ireland.

[[Okafor]] Same method. Manual strangulation, marks of fingers on the neck, no other injury described. In William's case a small child. In Henry's case a healthy adult man of twenty-five. That's a different matter. To strangle a fit adult by hand and leave no other mark, no blows, no defence injuries that anyone thought worth mentioning, you need a great deal of strength, or a great deal of surprise. I can't tell you which. I can tell you it's the same signature.

And here's the thing about that signature. Victor has an alibi for both, but they aren't the same kind of alibi. For Henry, it's the best kind: an Irish grand jury of strangers, with witnesses collected by Mr Kirwin, found that Victor was on an island in the Orkneys. For William, it's only Victor's word: a walking tour with Henry around Ingolstadt, and a letter from his father that fits the dates. I believe it. But a court never tested it.

Whoever kills with their hands on the throat, a grand jury has said at least once that it isn't Victor.

---

But there are two things in this episode I can't close, and I promised I'd tell you when that happens.

The first is the boats. Daniel Nugent saw a single man in a boat that looked like Victor's. Victor says the creature left Orkney in a boat, heading for the mainland. So on the coast that night there may have been two men alone in two small boats, and a man on a dark beach, by starlight, couldn't have told them apart. It could have been either. I don't know.

The second is worse. Henry Clerval was in Perth. Victor had a letter from him saying so, the day before. Perth is in Scotland. Henry's body was found on a beach in Ireland, not cold, the night before Victor landed there. How did Henry get to Ireland? Who took him? Did he go willingly? The letters don't say. Victor never asks. Nobody in the file asks. I've read every line twice and it isn't there.

[[Judith]] I asked myself the same thing. I even looked at a map, which I'm sure you've done. It doesn't make any sense, unless someone brought him. And if someone brought him, then they had a boat, and a reason, and a few days, and nobody saw.

So that's open. It's going to stay open.

---

Here's where we are. Henry Clerval, who nursed Victor through his first collapse and followed him to Scotland, was found strangled on an Irish beach with the marks of fingers on his neck. Victor was accused, then cleared by a grand jury on an alibi. The creature had threatened him days before.

And Victor's reading of that threat, "I shall be with you on your wedding-night", was that the creature would come for him. So he went home to Geneva, and he married Elizabeth Lavenza. And on the night of the wedding, he carried a pistol.
