[[Walton]] Such a man has a double existence: he may suffer misery and be overwhelmed by disappointments, yet when he has retired into himself, he will be like a celestial spirit that has a halo around him, within whose circle no grief or folly ventures.

A sea captain wrote that, in the Arctic, about the dying man he had pulled off the ice. He meant it kindly. He meant a man with a rich inner life.

I'm Tessa Lind. Last season, I spent a year checking that captain's letters against every record I could find. The letters describe a young scientist from Geneva, Victor Frankenstein, who says he built a man and gave it life, and that the thing he made killed the people he loved. I went into the archives, and record after record, the letters were right. I told you I believed them.

Then a letter came. From a psychiatrist in London, who had read every page.

[[Kessler]] I'm not writing to tell you you're wrong. I'm writing to ask whether you've ever read the letters as if the man on the ice was the only one there.

[grave] So I went back to the beginning. To a student who worked two years without rest, in rooms full of things taken from graves. To a fever that lasted months. To a little boy on a plain in Geneva, a young woman on a scaffold, a young man on an Irish beach. And every time, I asked one question. Who else saw it?

The answers are not what I expected. Again and again, the only witness is Victor.

But not every time. There are three moments in these letters that this theory can't explain, and I'm not going to hide them from you. I'll give you every one. By the end, I'll have three doors open in front of me. Victor alone. Victor with someone. Or the story exactly as he told it.

[[Judith]] I've had these letters in my family for two hundred years. I thought I knew what they said. Mind you, I thought that last year too.
