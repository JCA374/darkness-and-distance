# Episode 7: Evian

[grave] [[Victor]] This night, and all will be safe; but this night is dreadful, very dreadful.

Victor Frankenstein, to his wife, on the night of their wedding, at an inn on the shore of Lake Geneva. There's a storm coming in. And under his coat, he's holding a pistol.

---

From a sea chest in Kent, this is Darkness and Distance, season two. I'm Tessa Lind.

Last time, the island and the beach. A face at the window that only Victor saw, and a promise, made in his own room: I shall be with you on your wedding night. Then Ireland, and Henry Clerval, strangled, the body still warm, at an hour when a grand jury found Victor was hundreds of miles away. The theory hit its wall. If Victor was the monster, he wasn't the whole of it.

This week, the night the theory is strongest. I want to tell you about Elizabeth first.

---

Elizabeth Lavenza was not born a Frankenstein. When Victor was a small boy, his parents met her on a trip to the Lake of Como, an orphan girl living with a poor family of foster parents, and they took her home to Geneva. The children called each other cousin. There was no blood between them.

When Victor was seventeen, Elizabeth caught scarlet fever. Victor's mother, Caroline, nursed her, against advice. Elizabeth recovered. Caroline caught it, and died. On her deathbed, Victor says, she joined their hands and said she'd always hoped they would marry.

After that, Elizabeth ran the house. She wrote to Victor in Ingolstadt about the family and the town, cheerfully, with all the news. She spoke for Justine in court when the other witnesses were too frightened to say much. She sat with Justine in prison for hours. She grieved for William and blamed herself for the miniature.

And when Victor was on his way home from Ireland, after the fever, she wrote him a letter I think is one of the bravest things in this whole record. She says she's afraid he's been unhappy for years because he feels bound to marry her. So she asks him straight out.

[grave] She writes, "Do you not love another?"

She tells him that she loves him. And she tells him that if he married her only out of honor, it would make her miserable for ever. "Be happy, my friend," she writes. She asks him not to answer tomorrow, or the next day, if it would hurt him. Only to answer honestly.

Victor answers by fixing the date of the wedding. And he tells her something else. "I have one secret, Elizabeth, a dreadful one," he writes. When she hears it, it will chill her with horror. He'll tell her the day after they're married.

The day after. I've wondered all season what that secret was. Last year I'd have said: the creature. This year I asked Dr Kessler.

[[Kessler]] He means the creature, I'm sure, in his own mind. But think what it would be like to be the person told. A man you've known since childhood sits you down the day after your wedding and tells you that he built a being out of the dead, and that it killed your little cousin and your friend. You'd have to decide, that morning, whether you'd married a man with a terrible secret or a very sick man. She never had to decide. I find that one of the saddest things in the whole file.

---

They were married in Geneva, and that afternoon they set out by boat across the lake, to spend the night at Evian, on the far shore, and travel on the next day. It was a beautiful day. Victor names the mountains they saw as they crossed. He says Elizabeth was quiet, and when he asked why, she told him she was happy, but that something whispered to her "not to depend too much on the prospect that is opened before us". Then she told him to look at the fish in the clear water. You could see every pebble on the bottom, she said. "What a divine day!"

They landed at eight in the evening, walked on the shore, and went into the inn. Then the wind rose, and a storm came down the lake.

And here's Victor. He tells us that as soon as it got dark, a thousand fears came over him. His right hand was on a pistol, hidden in his coat. Every sound terrified him. He'd decided to sell his life dearly. Elizabeth watched him, frightened, and asked what was wrong.

He spent an hour like that. And then he had an idea. If there was going to be a fight, he didn't want her to see it. So he asked her to go to bed.

[[Victor]] She left me, and I continued some time walking up and down the passages of the house and inspecting every corner that might afford a retreat to my adversary.

He found nothing. He was beginning to think, he says, that something had stopped the creature coming.

[grave] [[Victor]] Suddenly I heard a shrill and dreadful scream. It came from the room into which Elizabeth had retired.

[grave] The scream came again. Victor ran into the room. Elizabeth was lying across the bed, dead.

---

[grave] I wanted to find her in the record, and last year I did. The parish of Evian was in Savoy then, not Geneva, and the death register is in a Savoy archive, in French, in a clerk's tidy hand. I went back this spring and read it again, because I wanted to hear it this time with the theory in my ears.

[[Archive]] Elisabeth Lavenza, wife of Victor Frankenstein of Geneva, found dead at the inn on the night of her marriage.

[grave] On the night of her marriage. I shall be with you on your wedding night. Victor had heard that sentence on the island and been certain it meant him. He'd carried a pistol for himself. He'd sent her away for her own safety. The night in the threat was the night she died.

---

When Victor came round, the people of the inn were around him. He went back to her. Someone had moved her, and laid a handkerchief over her face and neck, so that she might have been asleep.

[grave] [[Victor]] The murderous mark of the fiend's grasp was on her neck, and the breath had ceased to issue from her lips.

And then, he says, he looked up. The shutters had been closed. Now they were open, and the moon was shining in. At the open window was a figure. It was grinning. It pointed at the body.

Victor pulled the pistol and fired. The figure leapt from the window and ran, he says, "with the swiftness of lightning", and plunged into the lake.

The shot brought a crowd. They took boats out. They cast nets. Then they searched the woods and the vineyards, in parties, for hours. They found nothing.

And Victor tells us what most of them thought.

[[Victor]] After passing several hours, we returned hopeless, most of my companions believing it to have been a form conjured up by my fancy.

---

I've been waiting all season to put this night to Dr Kessler, and I could tell she'd been waiting too.

[[Kessler]] I'll make the case properly, because it deserves to be made properly, and then I'll tell you what it costs. On this night, Victor is the only man in the building who has been told something is coming. He's armed. He's in, by his own description, a state of extreme fear. He sends his wife to a room alone. He's alone himself, in the passages, for an unknown time. Nobody can say where he was standing when she screamed. Then he's found on the floor, senseless. Then he sees a figure at the window that nobody else sees, fires at it, and the searchers, the people who were actually there, mostly conclude he imagined it.

I asked about the marks on her neck. I'd asked Dr Okafor that too.

[[Okafor]] The same as William and Henry. The mark of a grasp at the throat. Manual strangulation. And I'll say what I said about William. Any adult could do it. A man of ordinary size could do it to a woman of ordinary size, quickly, especially if she's taken by surprise. The marks don't tell you the hand was large. Nobody measured them.

So I asked Dr Kessler what the theory costs.

[[Kessler]] It costs this. It asks you to believe that Victor Frankenstein killed the person he loved most in the world, with his own hands, on his wedding night, and didn't know it. That's a very large thing to ask. In my working life I saw people do things they had no memory of. It's rare. It's real. But I never once found it on the evidence of a single night. You'd want much more than this.

Here's why I believe the theory has its strongest night here. Not because of the window. Because of the searchers. Last season I told you they were wrong. This season I have to tell you that they were the only other witnesses we have to what happened at Evian, and most of them, standing in the rain at the edge of the lake with nets, thought the figure was in Victor's head.

---

And here's what I can't get past. He got the threat wrong.

On the theory, the threat on the island came from Victor's own mind. If so, his mind knew what it was planning. And yet the waking Victor, by every sign we have, misread it completely. From the moment it got dark, he was sure he would die. He armed himself against an attack on himself. He sent Elizabeth away to keep her safe. And the scream came from her room while he was looking in corners for someone who wanted him.

Dr Kessler says that's exactly what the theory would predict, that one part of him wouldn't know what the other part meant. I understand that. But it's the part of this I find hardest to believe. It means that the man in the passages with the pistol was protecting her from himself, and didn't know it.

And there's the other door, still open from Ireland. If someone else was at Evian that night, someone with a boat, or someone who could swim, then the figure at the window was real, and the searchers missed it in a storm on a black lake.

I don't know where Victor was standing when she screamed. Nobody does. I'm leaving that open.

---

[grave] Victor went home. His father, Alphonse, was alive, and so was his brother Ernest. But Alphonse couldn't bear the news. Elizabeth had been his more than daughter. Soon he couldn't get up from his bed. And in a few days, he died in Victor's arms.

And then, Victor says, he stopped knowing what happened to him. Chains, he remembers. And darkness. Sometimes dreams of walking in meadows with the friends of his youth, and then waking in a dungeon. Slowly the world came back. And when it did, he understood where he'd been.

I asked Dr Reber, in Geneva, whether a city as careful about paper as his would have written it down. He was quiet on the phone for a moment.

[[Reber]] If the son of a former syndic was confined, there will be a record. I will look.

He found it three weeks later, in the city's register of persons confined by its order. He sent me the photograph, with a note: I thought you should read it yourself.

[[Archive]] Victor Frankenstein, son of the late Monsieur Alphonse Frankenstein. Received, of disordered mind. To be kept apart.

[grave] [[Victor]] For they had called me mad, and during many months, as I understood, a solitary cell had been my habitation.

Next time, the last episode of the season. Victor gets out. He goes to a judge and accuses the creature of everything. The judge doesn't believe him. And Victor leaves Geneva for good, to hunt the thing he says he made, across half the world, all the way to the ice.

This has been Darkness and Distance. I'm Tessa Lind.
