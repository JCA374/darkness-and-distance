# Case bible: *Darkness and Distance*, season two: *A Double Existence*

Season two retells the same case from the Walton letters, with a new main track: **Victor Frankenstein, broken by
his work, may have been the monster himself**, a second self he could not see. The season builds that case
honestly and keeps three doors open to the end: **Victor alone**, **Victor with an accomplice**, **a real creature**.

*The listener never hears this file. Season one's bible (`case/bible.md`) still holds: rules 1–24 apply unchanged.
This file adds season two's premise, the rules for the theory, the twists, the new guest and the knowledge states.*

## Premise

A year after season one, Tessa Lind gets a letter from a listener: Dr Marian Kessler, a forensic psychiatrist in
London. She has read the letters on the season-one site. Her question: "Have you ever read the letters as if the man
on the ice was the only one there?" Tessa believed in season one, and said so. She owes the other reading a fair
hearing, so she goes back through the whole case, in the order of Victor's own life this time: Ingolstadt, the
fever, the deaths, the cell in Geneva, the ice.

The host's tone stays wonder, now pointed at a darker possibility: "Could it really be him?" She builds the case for
the theory where the documents carry it, and every episode she meets something that pushes back. By the finale the
theory explains almost everything, except the first page of the letters and the last, both written by Walton with
Victor nowhere near the pen.

The book's events and outcome are unchanged, exactly as in season one. Nobody in the podcast ever says Victor killed
anyone. The theory is a reading, voiced as a question and as labelled opinion.

## Rules for the theory (S1–S10)

S1. **A reading, never a finding.** The host never states that Victor killed anyone, was the creature, or had a
    "split personality". She says what the theory would explain and where it breaks. Kessler gives opinion, labelled.
S2. **No diagnosis of the dead.** Kessler says early (ep01) that she cannot diagnose a man dead two hundred years. She
    uses the popular phrase "split personality" only to correct it: today's term is dissociative identity disorder,
    rare and debated. She talks about what the documents describe: fevers, ravings, seeing what others don't, losing
    time. Once in the season (ep02) she says that people with mental illness are far more often victims than
    perpetrators of violence.
S3. **The theory may only use what is in the book.** It rests on Victor's own words (fevers, the spectre, "my own
    vampire, my own spirit", "I, not in deed, but in effect, was the true murderer", the ravings, "they had called me
    mad"), on Walton's ("wildness, and even madness", "a double existence", Victor's corrections), and on gaps (only
    Victor sees the figure at Plainpalais, Orkney and Evian). It never invents an event to fit.
S4. **The three doors stay open.** Every episode has one beat that pushes toward the theory (the belief beat) and one
    that pushes back (the nag). The nags carry the season's doubt and must be real: the crew's sighting, the trial
    clerk's "steps", the Felix and Safie letters and the Paris papers, the grand jury's Orkney alibi, the hamlet's
    giant, and the cabin.
S5. **The accomplice is never named.** The book names no accomplice. The podcast may ask "was someone with him?"
    (Ireland, the barn's steps, the Felix and Safie letters, the second boat) and never offers a name or a candidate.
    In the finale the second door is described only as a shape that fits the pages (a living, very large person who was
    there when Victor was not), labelled as not in the documents (fresh-listener note: door two needed substance).
S6. **Independent evidence is graded, as in season one (rule 10).** Things that do not pass through Victor: the
    sledge sighting (E02; E88: seen before Victor was aboard, written before he told his story, never corrected by him), the Felix and Safie letters Walton held (E63),
    the Paris papers (E82), the grand jury's finding (E47), the cabin (E70–E72, and E96: no second ink). The theory
    has to answer each one and says plainly when it can't.
S7. **Corrections are physical, not interpretive.** E62 (season one) says the second ink is heaviest in the
    creature's speeches. The conservator's count (E92) quantifies it. Nobody claims to know *what* Victor changed,
    only *where*. Walton's own words say he gave "the life and spirit" to the conversations.
S8. **The creature's crimes are told as his statement,** as in season one, with the same respect. The theory asks
    who wrote the words; it does not mock them.
S9. **Victims first.** William, Justine, Henry and Elizabeth get a life before their deaths, again. Victor gets
    context, not glamour, and the theory never makes his illness a spectacle.
S10. **No year,** as before.
